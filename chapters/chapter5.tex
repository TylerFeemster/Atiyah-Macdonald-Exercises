Since $\Omega$ is an integral domain, $f(A) \cong A/\ker(f)$ is also an integral domain. Thus, $\mathfrak{p} := \ker(f)$ is a prime ideal. By Theorem 5.10,

Let $\Sigma$ be the set of pairs $(C,\sigma)$, where $A \subset C \subset B$ and $\sigma : C \to \Omega$ is an embedding such that $\sigma$ restricts to $f$
on $A$. We can put a partial ordering on $\Sigma$ by letting $(C,\sigma) \le (C',\sigma')$ if $C \subset C'$ and $\sigma'$ restricts to $\sigma$ on $C$. Then,
$\Sigma$ is non-empty since it contains $(A,f)$. If we have a chain $(C_i,\sigma_i)$ of $\Sigma$, then $C = \bigcup_i C_i \subset B$ and $\sigma$ is defined on
$C$ by letting $\sigma(c) = \sigma_i(c)$ when $c \in C_i$; $c \in C$ implies $c \in C_i$ for some $i$, and this is well-defined by the compatibility requirement on 
restriction. Note that $\sigma$ is injective; if $\sigma(a) = \sigma(b)$, then since we're in a chain, they are identical on some common $i$, implying $a=b$ by the injectivity 
of each $\sigma_i$. Thus, $(C,\sigma) \in \Sigma$, so by Zorn's lemma, $\Sigma$ has maximal elements. If we have a maximal element of the form $(B,\sigma)$, we are
done, so suppose we have a maximal element $(C,\sigma)$, where $C \neq B$.

Suppose to the contrary that there is some $b \in B-C$; by assumption, $b$ is integral over $A$ and hence over $C$. Let $b$ satisfy $p(x) = \sum_i c_i x^i \in C[x]$.
Write $(\sigma p)(x) = \sum_i \sigma(c_i)x^i \in \Omega[x]$. Then, the composition
$$C[x] \to \Omega[x] \to \Omega[x]/((\sigma p)(x)) \cong \Omega$$
has kernel $(p(x))$, so we have an injection $C[x]/(p(x)) \to \Omega$ restricting to $\sigma$ on $C$. But $C[x]/(p(x)) \cong C[b]$, and by assumption
$C \subset C[b] \subset B$, where the first inclusion is proper. Thus, the existence of the induced map $C[b] \to \Omega$ violates the maximality of $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
Consider simple elements of $B' \otimes_A C$, i.e. elements of the form $b' \otimes c$, where $b' \in B'$ and $c \in C$. Since $B'$ is integral over $f(B)$
by assumption, there is some $p(x) = x^n + f(b_{n-1}) x^{n-1} + \cdots + f(b_0)$ with $b_i \in B$ such that $p(b') = 0$. Thus,
\begin{align*}
0 &= p(b') \\
0 &= p(b') \otimes c^n \\
0 &= (b')^n \otimes c^n + f(b_{n-1}) (b')^{n-1} \otimes c^n + \cdots + f(b_0) \otimes c^n \\
0 &= (b' \otimes c)^n + (f(b_{n-1}) \otimes c^1) (b' \otimes c)^{n-1} + \cdots + f(b_0) \otimes c^n.
\end{align*}
Thus, $b' \otimes c$ is integral over $f(B) \otimes C$, so every simple element of $B' \otimes C$ is integral over $f(B) \otimes C$.

In general, an element $x \in B' \otimes C$ is a finite sum of simple elements of $B' \otimes C$. By Corollary 5.3, the elements of
$B' \otimes C$ integral over $f(B) \otimes C$ form a subring of $B' \otimes C$. Since $x$ is a finite sum of simple elements, each of which is 
integral over $f(B) \otimes C$, $x$ is itself integral over $f(B) \otimes C$. Thus, $f \otimes 1 : B \otimes_A C \to B' \otimes_A C$ is integral
whenever $f$ is integral.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(i) Proof.]
Since $x \in A$ is a unit in $B$, there is some $b \in B$ such that $xb = 1$. $B$ is integral over $A$, so there is some polynomial of
minimal degree $n > 0$ such that 
$$b^n + a_{n-1}b^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 b + a_0 = 0,$$
where $a_i \in A$. Assume to the contrary that $n > 1$. Multiplying by $x$, we obtain
$$(xb)b^{n-1} + a_{n-1} (xb) b^{n-2} + \cdots + a_1 (xb) + a_0 x = 0,$$
but since $xb = 1$,
$$b^{n-1} + a_{n-1} b^{n-2} + \cdots + a_2 b + (a_1 + a_0 x) = 0.$$
Since $x \in A$, $a_1 + a_0 x \in A$, so this is a monic polynomial of $b$ (since $n > 1$) with coefficients in $A$. Since we chose $n > 1$ 
minimal, we clearly have a contradiction, so $n = 1$. In particular, $b$ satisfies a monic polynomial of the form $f(t) = t - a = 0$,
where $a \in A$, so $b = a \in A$, as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(ii) Proof.]
Let $\mathfrak{R}_B$ and $\mathfrak{R}_A$ be the Jacobson radicals of $B$ and $A$ respectively. We wish to show that $A \cap \mathfrak{R}_B = \mathfrak{R}_A$.
We will make use of Proposition 1.9, that $x \in \mathfrak{R} \iff 1 - xy$ is a unit in $R$ for all $y \in R$.

If $x \in \mathfrak{R}_B \cap A$, then $x \in A$ and $1 - xy$ is a unit in $B$ for all $y \in B$. Since $A \subset B$, $1 - xy$ is a unit in $B$
for all $y \in A$. In this case, $1 - xy \in A$, so by (i), since $1 - xy$ is a unit in $B$, it is also a unit in $A$. Thus, $1 - xy$ is a unit
in $A$ for all $y \in A$, so $x \in \mathfrak{R}_A$.

Conversely, suppose $x \not\in \mathfrak{R}_B \cap A$. If $x \not\in A$, then clearly $x \not\in \mathfrak{R}_A$, as desired. So, assume $x \in A$
so that $x \not\in \mathfrak{R}_B$. As $\mathfrak{R}_B$ is the intersection of maximal ideals of $B$, there is some maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of
$B$ such that $x \not\in \mathfrak{m}$. Since $\mathfrak{m}$ is prime, $\mathfrak{m}^c$ is also a prime ideal, so there is some maximal ideal 
$\mathfrak{m}_A$ of $A$ such that $\mathfrak{m}_A \supset \mathfrak{m}_B^c$. By Theorem 5.11 (``Going-Up''), there is a prime ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ such that
$\mathfrak{q} \supset \mathfrak{m}$ and $\mathfrak{q} \cap A = \mathfrak{m}_A$. Since $\mathfrak{m}$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{m}$, so 
$\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m}^c$. Thus, $\mathfrak{m}^c$ is maximal. Because $\mathfrak{m}^c = A \cap \mathfrak{m}$, $x \not\in \mathfrak{m}$
implies $x \not\in \mathfrak{m}^c$. Then, since $\mathfrak{m}^c$ is maximal, $x \not\in \mathfrak{R}_A$.
\end{proof}

\end{proof}

\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(i) Proof.]
Let $f,g \in B[x]$ be monic polynomials such that $fg \in C[x]$. Since $C$ is the integral closure of $A$ in $B$, $C$ is integrally closed in $B$ 
(Corollary 5.5). Since $B$ is an integral domain, it is a subring of its field of fractions, $K$ (Exercise \ref{3.9}). Thus, $C \subset B \subset K$,
so $fg \in K[x]$. Let $L$ be the splitting field of $fg$ over $K$; since $K \subset L$, $fg \in L[x]$ and splits into linear factors over $L[x]$. Thus,
$f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n(x - \xi_i) \in L[x]$ and $g(x) = \prod_{j=1}^m(x - \eta_j) \in L[x]$. Since $\{\xi_i,\eta_j\}_{i,j}$ are roots of the polynomial
$fg \in C[x]$, then $\{\xi_i, \eta_j\}_{i,j} \subset L$ are all integral over $C$.

Let $E$ be the integral closure of $C$ in $L$. Then, $\{\xi_i, \eta_j\}_{i,j} \subset E$. Since $E$ is a subring of $L$ (Corollary 5.3), the coefficients of
$f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n(x - \xi_i)$ and $g(x) = \prod_{j=1}^m(x - \eta_j)$ are all in $E$. Thus, $f(x),g(x) \in B[x] \cap E[x]$ ($B$ and $E$ viewed as 
subrings of $L$). Since $C$ is integrally closed in $B$, $C = B \cap E$, so $B[x] \cap E[x] = C[x]$. As desired, $f(x),g(x) \in C[x]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(ii) Proof.]
Let $f,g \in B[x]$ be monic polynomials such that $fg \in C[x]$.

First, we will show that there is a ring $B'$ containing $B$ such that $f$ and $g$ split into linear factors. Let $\deg(f) = n$. Let's extend $B$
by first creating the ring $D = B[x]/(f(x))$. In $D$, $f$ has the root $\overline{x}$, which we will call $\alpha$. Now, consider the canonical map
$D[y] \to D[y]/(y - \alpha)$. Since $B \subset D$, $f \in D[y]$. Note that $f(y) \mapsto \overline{f(y)} = \overline{f(\alpha)} = 0$, so 
$f(y) \in (y - \alpha)$. Thus, there is some polynomial $f_1 \in D[y]$ such that $f(y) = f_1(y)(y - \alpha)$. Suppose $b y^m$ is the term of highest
degree of $f_1(y)$. Then, the term of highest degree of the right-hand product is $b y^{m+1}$. The term of highest degree on the left-hand side is $y^n$,
so $m = n-1$ and $b = 1$. Thus, $f_1$ is a monic polynomial of degree $n - 1$. Repeating this process finitely, we obtain a ring $D'$ containing $B$ such
that $f(x)$ splits into linear factors in $D[x]$. Repeat the process again for $g$, starting with the ring $D$. Then, we obtain a ring $B'$ containing $B$
such that $f$ and $g$ both split into linear factors in $B'[x]$.

In particular, $f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n(x - \xi_i) \in B'[x]$ and $g(x) = \prod_{j=1}^m(x - \eta_j) \in B'[x]$. Note that $\{\xi_i,\eta_j\}_{i,j}$ are all
roots of $fg$ in $B'$, so each of these elements is a member of the integral closure $C'$ of $C$ in $B'$. Since $C'$ is a subring of $B'$ (Corollary 5.3),
the coefficients of the expansion of $f$ and $g$ are also in $C'$. Recall that by assumption the coefficients of $f$ and $g$ are in $B$. Thus, the coefficients
are in $B$ and are integral over $C$. However, $C$ is integrally closed in $B$, so the coefficients of $f$ and $g$ are in $C$. Thus, $f,g \in C[x]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Note that we've already shown $x,y \in C$ implies $xy \in C$ and $x + y \in C$. Here, we obtain a converse. Consider the polynomial $(t - x)(t - y) \in C[t]$
since $xy, x+y \in C$. By this result, $t-x,t-y \in C[t]$, so $x,y \in C$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $f \in B[x]$ is integral over $A[x]$ so that
$$f^n + g_{n-1} f^{n-1} + \cdots + g_0 = 0,$$

$$p^n + h_{n-1} p^{n-1} + \cdots + h_0 = 0,$$

$$p^n + h_{n-1} p^{n-1} + \cdots + h_1 p^1 = p(p^{n-1} + h_{n-1} p^{n-2} + \cdots + h_1) \in C[x].$$

$B[x]$ is contained in $C[x]$.

Conversely, to show that any element of $C[x]$ is integral over $A[x]$, it is enough to consider monomials in $C[x]$ since any element
of $C[x]$ is a sum of monomials (and the integral closure of $A[x]$ in $B[x]$ forms a ring). The monomials of $C[x]$ have the form $c x^n$, 
where $c \in C$ and $n \ge 0$. Note that $c$ is integral over $A[x]$ since it is integral over $A$ via the definition of $C$. Further, $x^n$ 
is integral over $A[x]$ since $x^n \in A[x]$. Thus, the product $cx^n$ is integral since the integral elements of $B[x]$ over $A[x]$ form a 
ring. Thus, $C[x]$ is the integral closure of $A[x]$ in $B[x]$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.12}

\begin{proof}
Note that we can extend the automorphism group $G$ of $A$ to $A[x]$ by defining $\sigma(x) = x$. Then clearly, $(A[x])^G = A^G[x]$ since for
an arbitary element of $A[x]$, we have
$$\sigma\left(a_n x^n + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0\right) = \sigma(a_n) x^n + \cdots + \sigma(a_1) x + \sigma(a_0).$$
For $a \in A$, consider the element $p_a(x) = \prod_{\sigma \in G}(x - \sigma(a))$. Since at least one linear term is $(x - a)$ ($G$ is a group, 
so it contains the trivial automorphism), $p_a(a) = 0$. Note that for any $\tau \in G$,
$$\tau(p_a(x)) = \tau\left(\prod_{\sigma \in G}(x - \sigma(a))\right) = \prod_{\sigma \in G}\tau(x - \sigma(a))
= \prod_{\sigma \in G}(x - (\tau\sigma)(a)) = \prod_{\sigma \in G}\tau(x - \sigma(a)) = p_a(x),$$
so $p_a(x) \in (A[x])^G = A^G[x]$. Lastly, $p_a(x)$ is clearly monic, so $a$ is integral over $A^G$; hence, $A$ is integral over $A^G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be a multiplicatively closed subset of $A$ such that $\sigma(S) \subset S$ for all $\sigma \in G$, and let $S^G = S \cap A^G$. We wish to
show that the action of $G$ on $A$ extends to an action on $S^{-1}A$. Since $S \subset A$, the obvious definition will be thus: for arbitrary 
$a/s \in S^{-1}A$, where $a \in A$ and $s \in S$, $\sigma(a/s) := \sigma(a)/\sigma(s)$. Since $\sigma(S) \subset S$, this action is closed. Now, we must
show that $\sigma$ is a ring homomorphism for all $\sigma \in G$. Note that $\sigma(1) = \sigma(1/1) = \sigma(1)/\sigma(1) = 1/1 = 1$, and 
$\sigma(0) = \sigma(0/1) = \sigma(0)/\sigma(1) = 0/1 = 0$. If $a/s, a'/s' \in S^{-1}A$, then
$$\sigma\left(\frac{a}{s}\frac{a'}{s'}\right) = \sigma\left(\frac{a a'}{s s'}\right) = \frac{\sigma(a a')}{\sigma(s s')}
= \frac{\sigma(a)\sigma(a')}{\sigma(s)\sigma(s')} = \frac{\sigma(a)}{\sigma(s)}\frac{\sigma(a')}{\sigma(s')}
= \sigma\left(\frac{a}{s}\right)\sigma\left(\frac{a'}{s'}\right)$$
and
$$\sigma\left(\frac{a}{s} + \frac{a'}{s'}\right) = \sigma\left(\frac{a s' + a' s}{s s'}\right)
= \frac{\sigma(a s' + a' s)}{\sigma(s s')} = \frac{\sigma(a) \sigma(s') + \sigma(a')\sigma(s)}{\sigma(s)\sigma(s')}$$
$$= \frac{\sigma(a)}{\sigma(s)} + \frac{\sigma(a')}{\sigma(s')} = \sigma\left(\frac{a}{s}\right) + \sigma\left(\frac{a'}{s'}\right),$$
so indeed the elements of $G$ are ring homomorphisms of $S^{-1}A$ onto itself. Compatibility is also easy to show:
$$\sigma\left(\tau\left(\frac{a}{s}\right)\right) = \sigma\left(\frac{\tau(a)}{\tau(s)}\right)
= \frac{\sigma(\tau(a))}{\sigma(\tau(s))} = \frac{(\sigma\tau)(a)}{(\sigma\tau)(s)} = (\sigma\tau)\left(\frac{a}{s}\right).$$
Thus, $G$ is a group of ring homomorphisms on $S^{-1}A$. Since $G$ is a group, all elements are invertible, so the elements are actually
ring automorphisms.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
Finally, we wish to show that $(S^G)^{-1}A^G \cong (S^{-1}A)^G$. For any $a \in A^G$, $\sigma(a/1) = \sigma(a)/1 = a/1$, so the sequence of
canonical maps $A^G \to A \to S^{-1}A$ factors as $A^G \to (S^{-1}A)^G \to S^{-1}A$. If $s \in S^G \subset S$, then $s$ becomes a unit in 
$S^{-1}A$, so it is a unit in $(S^{-1}A)^G$ since $1/s$ is $G$-fixed (as $s$ is). In particular, we can invoke the universal property of
localizations (Proposition 3.1) to obtain a unique homomorphism $\psi : (S^G)^{-1}A^G \to (S^{-1}A)^G$ that takes $a/s \mapsto a/s$.

To show that $\psi$ is bijective, we start by showing it is injective. Suppose $a \in A^G, s \in S^G$, and $\psi(a/s) = 0$. Then, $a/s = 0/1$ in 
$(S^{-1}A)^G$, so there is some $t \in S$ such that $ta = 0$. Since $S$ is $G$-stable and multiplicatively closed, and since $G$ is finite, 
$t' = \prod_{\sigma \in G}\sigma(t) \in S^G$, and $t'a = 0$. Since $t' \in S^G$, we have in $(S^G)^{-1}A^G$ that $a/s = (at')/(st') = 0/(st') = 0$,
so $\psi$ is injective.

To show surjectivity, suppose $a/s \in (S^{-1}A)^G$. Let $s' = \prod_{\sigma \in G : \sigma \neq \id} \sigma(s)$ so that $ss' = \prod_{\sigma \in G} \sigma(s)$.
By assumption, $a/s$ is $G$-fixed, and clearly, $ss'/1$ is fixed since the action of $G$ on the product $ss'$ simply permutes the factors. Thus, their product,
$(a/s)(ss'/1) = (as')/1$ is $G$-fixed. Thus, for all $\sigma \in G$, $as'/1 = \sigma(as'/1) = \sigma(as')/1$, so there is some $t_{\sigma} \in S$ such that
$t_{\sigma}(as' - \sigma(as')) = 0$. Let $t = \prod_{\tau \in G}\tau\left(\prod_{\sigma \in G}t_{\sigma}\right) \in S^G$. Since $t_{\sigma}$ is a factor of $t$,
we have $\sigma(t a s') = t \sigma(a s') = t a s'$. Thus, $a/s = (t a s')/(t s' s)$, where $tas' \in A^G$, and $t s' s \in S^G$ since $t \in S^G$ and 
$s' s \in S^G$. Thus, $\psi$ is surjective.
\end{proof}

$$A^G \cap \sigma(\mathfrak{p}_2) = \sigma^{-1}(A^G \cap \sigma(\mathfrak{p}_2)) 
= A^G \cap (\sigma^{-1}\sigma)(\mathfrak{p}_2) = A^G \cap \mathfrak{p}_2 = \mathfrak{p},$$

Hence, $G$ acts transitively on $P$; in particular, $P$ is finite since $G$ is finite.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.15}

\begin{proof}
$A$ is an integrally closed domain, $K$ is its field of fractions, $L$ is a finite extension field of $K$, and $B$ is the integral
closure of $A$ in $L$. Let $L_{sep}$ be the maximal separable subfield of $K$ in $L$, and let $C$ be the integral closure of $A$ in 
$L_{sep}$. Note that $L/L_{sep}$ is purely inseparable, and $L_{sep}/K$ is separable. To show that the fibers of $\Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)$
are finite, it is clearly sufficient to show that the fibers of $\Spec(B) \to \Spec(C)$ and $\Spec(C) \to \Spec(A)$ are each finite.

Since $L_{sep}/K$ is a separable extension, we can embed $L_{sep}$ in a finite normal, separable extension $E$ of $K$. Let $G$ be the 
Galois group of $E/K$, and let $D$ be the integral closure of $E$ in $A$ (note that $C \subset D$). By Exercise \ref{5.14}, $A = D^G$,
so by Exercise \ref{5.13}, $\Spec(D) \to \Spec(D^G) = \Spec(A)$ has finite fibers. Since we can factor this as
$\Spec(D) \to \Spec(C) \to \Spec(A)$, $\Spec(C) \to \Spec(A)$ has finite fibers.

Now, we must consider the purely inseparable extension $L/L_{sep}$. If the characteristic of $L_{sep}$ is $0$, $L_{sep} = L$ so that
$C = B$. Clearly then, $\Spec(B) \to \Spec(C)$ has finite fibers. So, suppose the characteristic of
$L_{sep}$ is $p > 0$. Let $\mathfrak{p} \in \Spec(C)$, and let $\mathfrak{q} \in \Spec(B)$ be such that $\mathfrak{q} \cap C = \mathfrak{p}$.
In a finite purely inseparable extension, every $\ell \in L$ has a power $p^n$ ($n \ge 0$) such that $\ell^{p^n} \in L_{sep}$. Let 
$\ell \in \mathfrak{q} \subset B \subset L$. Then, for some $n \ge 0$, $\ell^{p^n} \in L_{sep}$. Since $\ell$ is integral over $A$, 
$\ell^{p^n}$ is integral over $A$ (integral elements form a subring), so $\ell^{p^n} \in B \cap L_{sep} = C$. But $\ell \in \mathfrak{q}$, so 
$\ell^{p^n} \in \mathfrak{q} \cap C = \mathfrak{p}$. Since $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, we have (by a simple inductive argument) that 
$\ell \in \mathfrak{p}$. Thus, $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}$, so the fibers of $\Spec(B) \to \Spec(C)$ are clearly finite.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.16}

Let $k$ be a field, and let $A \neq 0$ be a finitely generated $k$-algebra. Then there exist elements $y_1,\ldots,y_r \in A$ which are
algebraically independent over $k$ and such that $A$ is integral over $k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$. (We will assume that $k$ is infinite, even
though it remains true when $k$ is finite).

\begin{proof}
$A$ is finitely generated, so let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ generate $A$ as a $k$-algebra. We can renumber the $x_i$ so that $x_1,\ldots,x_r$
are algebraically independent over $k$ and each of $x_{r+1},\ldots,x_n$ is algebraic over $k[x_1,\ldots,x_r]$. Now we proceed by induction
on $n$. If $n = r$ there is nothing to do; this is the base case: $k[x_1,\ldots,x_r]$ is clearly integral over itself. So suppose $j > r$ 
and assume the result is true for $j - 1$ generators, i.e. $k[z_1,\ldots,z_{j-1}]$ is integral over $k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$ for generators 
$z_1,\ldots,z_{j-1}$, where $y_1,\ldots,y_r$ are a collection of algebraically independent elements over $k$. The generator $x_j$ is algebraic 
over $k[x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1}]$, so there is a non-zero polynomial $f \in k[x_1,\ldots,x_j]$ such that $f(x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_j) = 0$. Let $F$ 
be the homogeneous part of highest degree in $f$. Since $k$ is infinite, there exist $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_{j-1} \in k$ such that 
$F(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_{j-1},1) \neq 0$. Let $x_i' = x_i - \lambda_i x_j$ so that $x_i = \lambda_i x_j + x_i'$.

Now, we'll show that $x_j$ is integral over $k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}']$. Let $a = F(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_{j-1},1) \neq 0$.
$$F(x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_j) = F(\lambda_1 x_j + x_1',\ldots, \lambda_{j-1} x_j + x_{j-1}', x_j) \in k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}'][x_j].$$
Since $F \in k[x_1,\ldots,x_j]$ is homogeneous, it has some homogeneous degree $m > 0$. Since we replace the arguments $x_1,\ldots,x_j$
with homogeneous polynomials of degree 1, the polynomial remains homogeneous of degree $m$. Collecting terms in $k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}'][x_j]$,
we have
$$F(\lambda_1 x_j + x_1',\ldots, \lambda_{j-1} x_j + x_{j-1}', x_j) = F_0(x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}') x_j^m + \cdots + F_m(x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}'),$$
where $F_i \in k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}']$ is homogeneous of degree $i$. Finally, observe that the term of highest degree satisfies
$$F_0 x_j^m = F(\lambda_1 x_j,\ldots, \lambda_{j-1} x_j, x_j) = F(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_{j-1}, 1)x_j^m = ax_j^m \neq 0,$$
as desired. Then, the term of highest degree in $x_j$ is $a^{-1}F(\lambda_1 x_j + x_1',\ldots, \lambda_{j-1} x_j + x_{j-1}', x_j)$ is $x_j^m$.

Remember that $f(x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_j) = 0$, so $a^{-1}f(x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_j) = 0$. Then, 
$0 = a^{-1}f(\lambda_1 x_j + x_1, \ldots, \lambda_{j-1}x_j + x_{j-1}', x_j) \in k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}'][x_j]$. Finally, note that since we replaced
arguments with degree 1 homogeneous polynomials, the highest degree terms are still $m$. As we just saw, the degree $m$ terms are 
$a^{-1}F(\lambda_1 x_j + x_1',\ldots, \lambda_{j-1} x_j + x_{j-1}', x_j)$, so when we collect terms in $k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}'][x_j]$,
$a^{-1}f(x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_j)$ is monic in $x_j$, so $x_j$ is integral over $k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}']$. 

In particular, $k[x_1,\ldots,x_j]$ is integral over $k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}']$. By the inductive hypothesis, we have that 
$k[x_1',\ldots,x_{j-1}']$ is integral over $k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$. By the transitivity of integrality, $A$ is integral over $k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$. 
By induction, $A$ is integral over $k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$ for algebraically independent elements $y_1,\ldots,y_r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is ``Noether's Normalization Theorem''.
\end{remark}

There is a geometric interpretation if $k$ is algebraically closed: If $X$ is an affine algebraic variety in $k^n$ with coordinate ring
$A \neq 0$, then there exists a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $r$ in $k^n$ and a linear mapping of $k^n$ onto $L$ which maps $X$ onto $L$.

\begin{proof}
The coordinate ring is given by $P(X) = k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/I(X)$, which is clearly finitely generated as a $k$-algebra. Indeed, 
$P(X) = k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, where $x_i$ is the coordinate function associated to $t_i$. By the previous proof, the collection of 
$y_1, \ldots, y_r$ we constructed was a sequence of linear combinations of $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and thus is itself a linear combination of 
the $x_1, \ldots, x_n$; here, we use the fact that $k$ is infinite (it is algebraically closed).

For each $i$ then, $y_i = \sum_{j=1}^n c_{ij} x_j$. Define $\varphi : k[y_1,\ldots,y_r] \to k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ to be the $k$-algebra homomorphism generated 
by $\varphi(y_i) = \sum_{j=1}^n c_{ij} t_j$. Let $y_i' = \varphi(y_i)$. Since $\{y_i\}$ are algebraically independent, $\{y_i'\}$ are also algebraically 
independent. Since $y_i'$ are each linear combinations of $\{t_j\}$, we can find and addend $y_{r+1}',\ldots,y_n' \in k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ to $\{y_i'\}_{i=1}^r$
so that $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n] \cong k[y_1',\ldots,y_n']$ as $k$-algebras. 

For every point $a = (a_1,\ldots,a_n) \in k^n$, we have a $k$-algebra homomorphism $\ell : k^n \to k^r$ given by $(y_1'(a),\ldots,y_r'(a))$. This is a $k$-linear
map since each $y_i'$ is a $k$-linear combination of $t_j$, so $y_i'(a) = \sum_{j=1}^n c_{ij} a_j$. Let $L$ be the $r$-dimensional subvariety of $k^n$ defined by 
$(y_{r+1}' = 0, \ldots, y_n' = 0)$. This is a linear subspace, as $y_i' = 0$ represents a linear equation in terms of $\{t_j\}$.

Consider the canonical $k$-algebra homomorphism $\pi : k[y_1',\ldots,y_n'] \to k[y_1',\ldots,y_n']/I(X) \cong P(X)$. Note that 
$k[y_1,\ldots,y_r] \cong k[y_1',\ldots,y_n']/(y_{r+1}' = 0,\ldots,y_n' = 0) = P(L)$, so $\tau : P(L) \xhookrightarrow{} P(X)$. 
In light of Exercise \ref{1.28}, we obtain a regular map $\psi : X \to L$.

Now, let $a = (a_1,\ldots,a_n) \in L$. Then, we have a $k$-algebra homomorphism $\theta_a : P(L) \to k$ defined by evaluating functions at $a$.
Since $P(X) \cong k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is integral over $P(L) \cong k[y_1,\ldots,y_r]$ by the previous result, and since $k$ is algebraically closed, 
we have by Exercise \ref{5.2} that $\theta_a$ can be extended to a $k$-algebra homomorphism $\hat\theta_a : P(X) \to k$. A $k$-algebra homomorphism 
from $P(X)$ to $k$ corresponds to a point $b \in X$ such that $\psi(b) = a$ (Exercise \ref{1.28}). Thus, $\psi$ is surjective.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.18}

\begin{proof}
Let $B$ be a finitely generated $k$-algebra, where $k$ is a field. Assume $B$ is itself a field. Let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ generate $B$ as a $k$-algebra.
We will prove that $B$ is a finite algebraic extension of $k$ by induction. If $n = 1$, then $B = k[x_1]$. Since $B$ is a field, $x_1^{-1} \in B$, so we
can write $x_1^{-1} = a_0 + a_1 x_1 \cdots + a_n x_1^n$, where $a_i \in k$ and $a_n \neq 0$. Then,
$$a_n x_1^{n+1} + \cdots + a_1 x_1^2 + a_0 x_1 - 1 = 0,$$
so $B = k[x_1]$ is a finite algebraic extension of $k$. This proves the base case.

Now, assume $n > 1$ and the inductive hypothesis holds for $m < n$, and assume $B$ has $n$ generators $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ as a $k$-algebra. Let $A = k[x_1]$
and $K = k(x_1)$, the field of fractions of $A$. $B$ is a finitely generated $K$-algebra with generators $x_2,\ldots,x_n$, so by the inductive hypothesis,
$B$ is a finite algebraic extension of $K$. Thus, $x_2,\ldots,x_n$ each satisfy a monic polynomial equation with coefficients in $K$, i.e. coefficients of
the form $a/b$, where $a,b \in A$. Let $f \in A$ be the product of all denominators over each monic polynomial. Then, $x_2,\ldots,x_n$ are integral over $A_f$,
as these same monic polynomials map canonically to $A_f[x]$. Since $B$ is generated as a ring by $x_2,\ldots,x_n$, $B$ is integral over $A_f$. Since $K \subset B$,
$K$ is also integral over $A_f$.

If $x_1$ is algebraic over $k$, then $K$ is a finite algebraic extension of $k$. Since $B$ is a finite algebraic extension of $K$, $B$ is a finite algebraic extension
of $k$ by transitivity, as desired. Then, the proof follows by induction. So, we only have to prove that $x_1$ is algebraic over $k$. Assume to the contrary that
$x_1$ is transcendental over $k$. Then, $A = k[x_1]$ is a unique factorization domain, so $A$ is integrally closed. By Proposition 5.12, since $A_f$ has the same
field of fractions $K$ as $A$, which is integrally closed, $A_f$ is itself integrally closed. But, we saw that $K$ is integral over $A_f$, so we must have $A_f = K$;
thus, $k(x_1) = k[x_1]$, showing that $x_1^{-1} \in k[x_1]$. By our proof of the base case above, this shows that $x_1$ is algebraic over $k$, giving the desired
contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is known as ``Zariski's Lemma''.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.19}

First, we prove that all maximal ideals of $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ are of the form $(t_1-a_1,\ldots,t_n-a_n)$, where $a \in k^n$.

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be a maximal ideal of $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ so that $B = k[t_1,\ldots,t_n] / \mathfrak{m}$ is both a field and a finitely
generated $k$-algebra. By Exercise \ref{5.18}, $B$ is a finite algebraic extension of $k$, so since $k$ is algebraically closed, we have
a $k$-algebra homomorphism $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n] \to k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/\mathfrak{m} \xlongrightarrow{\sym} k$. Thus, for each $t_i$, we have
$t_i \mapsto a_i \in k$, so the kernel of this map is $(t_1-a_1,\ldots,t_n-a_n)$. But also, the kernel is clearly $\mathfrak{m}$, so
$\mathfrak{m} = (t_1-a_1,\ldots,t_n-a_n)$.
\end{proof}

We use this to show that if $X$ is an affine algebraic variety such that $I(X) \neq (1)$, then $X$ is non-empty.

\begin{proof}
If $I(X) \neq (1)$, then there is some maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ such that $\mathfrak{m} \supset I(X)$. We've just
shown that all maximal ideals are of the form $(t_1 - a_1, \ldots, t_n - a_n)$ for some $a \in k^n$, so $\mathfrak{m} = \mathfrak{m}_a$. But,
$\mathfrak{m}_a = I(\{a\})$, where the point $\{a\}$ is the affine algebraic variety associated to $\{t_i - a_i\}$. Since $I(\{a\}) \supset I(X)$,
we have the natural $k$-algebra homomorphism $P(X) \xhookrightarrow{} P(\{a\})$. By Exercise \ref{1.27}, there is some regular function $f : \{a\} \to X$,
so $X$ is non-empty as $f(a) \in X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}

$$z_i^k + f_{i,k-1} z_i^{k-1} + \cdots + f_{i,0} = 0,$$
where $f_{i,j} \in K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ for each $j$. Each $f_{i,j}$ is the sum of finitely many monomials of the form $c_{ij,\nu}x_1^{\nu_1}\cdots x_n^{\nu_n}$,

$$0 = s_i^k(z_i^k + f_{i,k-1} z_i^{k-1} + \cdots + f_{i,0}) = (s_i z_i)^k + s_i f_{i,k-1} (z_i s_i)^{k-1} + \cdots + f_{i,0}s_i^k.$$
Since $s_i$ was defined to clear denominators, this is the image in $K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ of $(s_i z_i)^k + s_i f_{i,k-1} (z_i s_i)^{k-1} + \cdots + f_{i,0}s_i^k \in A[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.

over $A[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ for each $i$. If $t = \prod_i s_i$, then $t z_i$ is integral over $A[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ for each $i$; letting $t_i = \prod_{j \neq i} s_j$, $t z_i = t_i s_i z_i$,

is integral over $A[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, $sz_i = qtz_i$ is integral over $A[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$. Further, since $s = q_i \cdot k_i$, where $k_i \in B$, we have
$x_i = (b_i k_i)/(q_i k_i) = y_i / s$, where $y_i = b_i k_i \in B$.

is integral over $B_s'$. Since the elements that generate $B_s$ are integral over $B_s'$, $B_s$ is integral over $B_s'$.
\end{proof}

ideal $\mathfrak{m} A_s$, its either all of $A_s$ or $\mathfrak{m} A_s$. But $s \in A_s$ and $\psi(s) \neq 0$, so $A_s[y_1,\ldots,y_n] \cap \ker \psi
= \mathfrak{m} A_s$.

Now, we wish to show that $B \cap \ker \psi$ is a maximal ideal of $B$. Let $b \in B$. Since $B$ is an integral domain, 
it injects into $(B_v)_s$, which is integral over $A_s[y_1,\ldots,y_n]$. Thus, $b$ satisfies an integral equation
$$b^k + a_{k-1}b^{k-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0,$$
where $a_i \in A_s[y_1,\ldots,y_n]$. Since $\psi : y_i \mapsto 0$, then $\psi(a_i) = \psi(c_i)$ for some $c_i \in A_s$. Then,
\begin{align*}
0 &= \psi(b)^k + \psi(a_{k-1})\psi(b)^{k-1} + \cdots + \psi(a_0) \\
&= \psi(b)^k + \psi(c_{k-1})\psi(b)^{k-1} + \cdots + \psi(c_0) \\
&= \psi(b^k + c_{k-1}b^{k-1} + \cdots + c_0).
\end{align*}
Then, $b^k + c_{k-1}b^{k-1} + \cdots + c_0 \in \ker \psi$. Modulo $\ker \psi$,
$$\overline{b}^k + \overline{c_{k-1}}\overline{b}^{k-1} + \cdots + \overline{c_0} = 0,$$

so by Proposition 5.7, $B / (B \cap \ker \psi)$ is a field, so $B \cap \ker \psi$ is a maximal ideal of $B$. Finally, since $\psi(v) \neq 0$, this maximal
ideal does not contain $v$. Thus, the Jacobson radical of $B$ is $0$ since no non-zero element of $B$ is contained in every maximal ideal.
\end{proof}

(ii) $\implies$ (i). In every homomorphic image of $A$, the nilradical is equal to the Jacobson radical. Let $\mathfrak{p} \in \Spec(A)$. Then,

(i) $\implies$ (ii). Suppose every prime in $A$ is the intersection of the maximal ideals containing it. Now, let $f : A \to B$ be a homomorphism of rings.
Then, $f(A)$ is itself a ring, and $f(A) \cong A / \ker f$. So, it is sufficient to show that for any ideal $\mathfrak{a}$, 
$\mathfrak{N}_{A/\mathfrak{a}} = \mathfrak{R}_{A/\mathfrak{a}}$. Note that the ideals of $A/\mathfrak{a}$ are precisely those of $A$ that contain $\mathfrak{a}$,
and this correspondence is order-preserving (Proposition 1.1). Let $\overline{\mathfrak{p}}$ be a prime ideal of $A/\mathfrak{a}$, so its contraction is a prime 
$\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ containing $\mathfrak{a}$. By assumption, $\mathfrak{p}$ is the intersection
of the maximal ideals containing it; note these maximal ideals must also contain $\mathfrak{a}$. Further, since the ideal correspondence is order-preserving, these
maximal ideals remain maximal in $A/\mathfrak{a}$, so $\overline{\mathfrak{p}}$ is an intersection of maximal ideals. Thus, the nilradical of $A/\mathfrak{a}$
is equal to the Jacobson radical, as desired.

(i) $\implies$ (iii). Suppose every prime ideal in $A$ is the intersection of maximal ideals, and suppose $\mathfrak{p} \in \Spec(A)$ is not maximal. Then, 
the prime ideals strictly containing $\mathfrak{p}$ includes all of the maximal ideals containing $\mathfrak{p}$. Thus, $\mathfrak{p}$ is a subset of
the intersection of primes strictly containing $\mathfrak{p}$, which in turn, is a subset of the maximal ideals containing $\mathfrak{p}$. Since 
this last intersection is $\mathfrak{p}$ by assumption, we have equality throughout, so $\mathfrak{p}$ is the intersection of primes strictly containing
$\mathfrak{p}$.

(iii) $\implies$ (ii). Assume (ii) is false, so (i) is false (we proved (i) $\implies$ (ii)). Therefore, there is some prime $\mathfrak{p}$ that is not
the intersection of the maximal ideals containing it. Thus, $B = A/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain with non-zero Jacobson radical $\mathfrak{R}$,
so let $f \in \mathfrak{R}$ be a non-zero element. Since $B$ is an integral domain, $B \to B_f$ is injective, so $B_f \neq 0$. Thus, $B_f$ has some
maximal ideal whose contraction in $B$ is a prime ideal $\mathfrak{q}$. Note that $f \not\in \mathfrak{q}$ (otherwise $(1/f)f = 1 \in \mathfrak{q}$).
Any prime ideal in $B$ that does not contain $f$ is in $B_f$ (Proposition 3.11), so since $\mathfrak{q}$ has this property and is maximal in $B_f$, it
is maximal in the set of primes of $B$ not containing $f$. Since $f$ is in every maximal ideal of $B$, $\mathfrak{q}$ is not maximal. Further, any prime
of $B$ containing $\mathfrak{q}$ strictly must contain $f$ since $\mathfrak{q}$ is maximal among primes that do not contain $f$. Thus, the intersection of
primes strictly containing $\mathfrak{q}$ contains $f$, so the intersection does not equal $\mathfrak{q}$. 

Finally, since $B = A/\mathfrak{p}$, $\mathfrak{q}$ lifts to a prime $\mathfrak{q}'$ of $A$. First, note that $\mathfrak{q}'$ is not maximal since $\mathfrak{q}$
is not maximal. Explicitly, there is some $\mathfrak{m}$ of $B$ such that $\mathfrak{m}$ strictly contains $\mathfrak{q}$, and this lifts to a maximal ideal
$\mathfrak{m}'$ that strictly contains $\mathfrak{q}'$; this is all in light of Proposition 1.1, which guarantees an order-preserving one-to-one correspondence
between ideals of $A$ containing $\mathfrak{p}$ and ideals of $A/\mathfrak{p}$. Finally, the intersection of primes of $A$ strictly containing $\mathfrak{q}'$ is
an ideal that maps to the intersection of primes of $A/\mathfrak{p}$ that strictly contain $\mathfrak{q}$. Since this latter intersection is not $\mathfrak{q}$,
the former intersection is not $\mathfrak{q}'$ (since the correspondence of ideals is one-to-one). Thus, (iii) is false, as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
A ring with these three equivalent properties is called a ``Jacobson ring''.
\end{remark}

(2) $\implies$ (3). The map $U \mapsto U \cap X_0$ that maps open sets of $X$ to open sets of $X_0$ is clearly surjective, as this is what defines open sets
of $X_0$. Now, suppose we have two open sets $U,V$ such that $U \cap X_0 = V \cap X_0$. Therefore, 
$$U^c \cap X_0 = X_0 - (U \cap X_0) = X_0 - (V \cap X_0) = V^c \cap X_0.$$ 
By assumption, every closed set $E$ of $X$ satisfies $\overline{E \cap X_0} = E$. Thus, we have
$V^c = \overline{V^c \cap X_0} = \overline{U^c \cap X_0} = U^c$, so $U = V$. Hence, the map is bijective.

(3) $\implies$ (1). Finally, assume the map $U \mapsto U \cap X_0$ from open sets of $X$ onto open sets of $X_0$ is bijective. Let $Y$ be a locally closed
subset of $X$, so $Y = U \cap C$, where $U$ is open and $C$ is closed. We will prove the contrapositive, so assume that $Y \cap X_0 = \emptyset$. Then, 
$U \cap C \cap X_0 = \emptyset$. Since $C$ is closed, $C^c$ is an open set. Then, 
$$U \cap X_0 = (U \cap C \cap X_0) \cup (U \cap C^c \cap X_0) = \emptyset \cup (U \cap C^c \cap X_0) = (U \cap C^c) \cap X_0.$$
By the bijectivity of the map (since $U \cap C^c$ is open), $U = U \cap C^c$, so $U \subset C^c$. Thus, $Y = U \cap C = \emptyset$. So, if a locally closed
set does not meet $X_0$, it is empty.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise - Complete}\label{5.27}

\begin{proof}
Let $K$ be a field and let $\Sigma$ be the set of local subrings of $K$. We can apply a partial order on $\Sigma$ using domination, i.e. $B$ dominates $A$
if $A \subset B$ and $\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m}_B \cap A$. Now, let $\mathcal{T}$ be a chain of elements of $\Sigma$. Define $R = \bigcup_{A \in \mathcal{T}} A$
and $\mathfrak{m} = \bigcup_{A \in \mathcal{T}}\mathfrak{m}_A$. We aim to prove that this is an upper bound of $\mathcal{T}$ in $\Sigma$.

First, note that $R$ is a subring of $K$ since it is a union of subrings of $K$. We want to show that $\mathfrak{m}$ is a maximal ideal of $R$, so we
need to show that it is an ideal. If $x, y \in \mathfrak{m}$, then there are rings $A_x , A_y \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_x}$ and
$y \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_y}$. Since $\mathcal{T}$ is a chain, either $A_x$ is a subring of $A_y$ or vice-versa. Assume without loss of generality that
$A_x \subset A_y$. Then, $\mathfrak{m}_{A_x} = \mathfrak{m}_{A_y} \cap A_x$, so $x \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_y}$ as well. Thus, $x + y \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_y} \subset \mathfrak{m}$.
Finally, if $r \in R$, then $r \in A_1$ for some $A_1 \in \mathcal{T}$. If $x \in \mathfrak{m}$, it is in $\mathfrak{m}_{A_2}$ for some ring $A_2 \in \mathcal{T}$.
Since $\mathcal{T}$ is a chain, either $A_1$ dominates $A_2$ or vice-versa. If $A_1 \subset A_2$, then $r \in A_2$, so $r x \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_2} \subset \mathfrak{m}$,
so $\mathfrak{m}$ is $R$-stable. If $A_2 \subset A_1$, then $x \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_2} \subset \mathfrak{m}_{A_1}$ so that $r x \in \mathfrak{m}_{A_1} \subset \mathfrak{m}$. Thus,
$\mathfrak{m}$ is an ideal of $R$.

We wish to show that for all $A \in \mathcal{T}$, $\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m} \cap A$. We have
$$A \cap \mathfrak{m} = A \cap \left(\bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{T}}\mathfrak{m}_B\right)
= A \cap \left(\bigcup_{B \le A}\mathfrak{m}_B \cup \bigcup_{B > A}\mathfrak{m}_B\right)$$
$$= \left(\bigcup_{B \le A}A \cap\mathfrak{m}_B\right) \cup \left(\bigcup_{B > A}A \cap \mathfrak{m}_B\right)
= \left(\bigcup_{B \le A}\mathfrak{m}_B\right) \cup \left(\bigcup_{B > A}\mathfrak{m}_A\right)
= \mathfrak{m}_A \cup \mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m}_A,$$
as desired. Now, suppose $I$ is an ideal of $R$ that properly contains the ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. Then, there is some $x \in I - \mathfrak{m} \subset R$,
so there is some $A \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in A$. Then, $I \cap A \supset \mathfrak{m} \cap A = \mathfrak{m}_A$ properly. Since $\mathfrak{m}_A$
is a maximal ideal of $A$, $I \cap A$ must be $A$. Thus, $1 \in I$, so $I = R$. In particular, $\mathfrak{m}$ is a maximal ideal of $R$.

We must show that $R$ is a local ring. Let $\mathfrak{m}'$ be an arbitrary maximal ideal of $R$. For any $A \in \mathcal{T}$, since $A$ has
a unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_A$, either $A \cap \mathfrak{m}' = (1)$ or $\mathfrak{m}_A \supset \mathfrak{m}' \cap A$. If there is any $A$
for which $A \cap \mathfrak{m}' = (1)$, then $1 \in \mathfrak{m}'$, so $\mathfrak{m}' = R$, violating maximality. Therefore, for all $A$,
we have $\mathfrak{m}_A \supset \mathfrak{m}' \cap A$. Taking the union over all $A$, we obtain $\mathfrak{m} \supset \mathfrak{m}' \cap R = \mathfrak{m}'$.
Since $\mathfrak{m}'$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{m}' = \mathfrak{m}$, so $R$ is indeed a local ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. Thus, $R \in \Sigma$.
Since $A \subset R$ and $\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m} \cap A$ for all $A \in \mathcal{T}$, $R$ is an upper bound of $\mathcal{T}$ in $\Sigma$. By Zorn's
lemma, $\Sigma$ has maximal elements.

Finally, we wish to show that $A \in \Sigma$ is maximal if and only if $A$ is a valuation ring of $K$. Let $R$ be a maximal element of $\Sigma$. Consider
the set of pairs $(A,f)$, where $A$ is a subring of $K$ and $f : A \to \Omega$ (an algebraically closed field) is a homomorphism. Then, the set $\Sigma'$ can be
ordered such that $(A,f) \le (B,g)$ if $A \subset B$ and $g$ restricts to $f$ on $A$. Prior to Lemma 5.19, the text proves that $\Sigma'$ satisifes Zorn's lemma
and thus has maximal elements. Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be the unique maximal ideal of $R$, and define $f : R \mapsto R/\mathfrak{m} \xhookrightarrow{} \Omega$, where
$\Omega$ is an algebraic closure of the residue field. Since $(R,f) \in \Sigma'$, there is some maximal element $(B,g)$ of $\Sigma'$ such that $(R,f) \le (B,g)$.
By Lemma 5.19, $B$ is a local ring and its maximal ideal is $\mathfrak{m}_B = \ker g$. Note that $R \cap \mathfrak{m}_B = R \cap \ker g = \ker f = \mathfrak{m}$.
Thus, $B$ dominates $R$, so we have $R \le B$ in $\Sigma$. Since $R$ is a maximal element of $\Sigma$, we have $(R,f) = (B,g)$. By Theorem 5.21, $B$ is a valuation
ring of $K$, so $R$ is a valuation ring of $K$.

Conversely, suppose $A$ is a valuation ring of $K$. Then, it is a local subring of $K$, so $A \in \Sigma$. Thus, there is some maximal element $B \in \Sigma$
so that $A \le B$. Since $A \subset B \subset K$, $B$ is also a valuation ring by Proposition 5.18. Since $B$ dominates $A$, $\mathfrak{m}_A = A \cap \mathfrak{m}_B$.
Note that $\mathfrak{m}_A$ and $\mathfrak{m}_B$ are the set of non-units of $A$ and $B$, respectively (Proposition 1.6). Let $b \in \mathfrak{m}_B$ (non-zero), so 
$b^{-1} \not\in B$. Thus, $b^{-1} \not\in A$, so since $A$ is a valuation ring of $K$, $b \in A$. Thus, $\mathfrak{m}_B \subset A$, so 
$\mathfrak{m}_A = A \cap \mathfrak{m}_B = \mathfrak{m}_B$. Finally, suppose $b \in B - \mathfrak{m}_B$. Then, there is an inverse $b^{-1} \in B$. Since $b^{-1}$
has its inverse $b$ in $B$, we must have $b^{-1} \in B - \mathfrak{m}_B$, as well. Now, since $A$ is a valuation ring of $K$, $b \in A$ or $b^{-1} \in A$. Swapping 
$b$ and $b^{-1}$ if necessary, we can assume $b \in A$. Since $\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m}_B$ and $b \not\in \mathfrak{m}_B$, $b \in A - \mathfrak{m}_A$. Thus, $b$
is a unit in $A$, so $b^{-1} \in A$, and as before, $b^{-1} \in A - \mathfrak{m}_A$ since $b^{-1}$ is a unit in $A$. Thus, $B - \mathfrak{m}_B \subset A - \mathfrak{m}_A$,
so that by $\mathfrak{m}_A = \mathfrak{m}_B$, $B \subset A$. Finally, $A = B$ so that $A$ is a maximal element of $\Sigma$, as desired.
\end{proof}
