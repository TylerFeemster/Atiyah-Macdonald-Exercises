\section{Noetherian Rings (Done)}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.1}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Sigma$ be the set of ideals in $A$ which are not finitely generated. Since $A$ is not Noetherian, $\Sigma$ is non-empty. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a chain
in $\Sigma$. Define $\mathfrak{a} = \bigcup_{\mathfrak{b} \in \mathcal{T}}\mathfrak{b}$. If $x_1,x_2 \in \mathfrak{a}$, then $x_1 \in \mathfrak{b}_1$ and $x_2 \in \mathfrak{b}_2$
for ideals $\mathfrak{b}_1,\mathfrak{b}_2 \in \mathcal{T}$. Since $\mathcal{T}$ is a chain, these ideals are comparable, so relabelling if necessary, we may assume that
$\mathfrak{b}_1 \subset \mathfrak{b}_2$. Then, $x_1,x_2 \in \mathfrak{b}_2$, so $x_1 + x_2 \in \mathfrak{b}_2 \subset \mathfrak{a}$. Thus, $\mathfrak{a}$ is additively closed.
If $a \in A$ and $x \in \mathfrak{a}$, there is some $\mathfrak{b} \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in \mathfrak{b}$. Then, $ax \in \mathfrak{b} \subset \mathfrak{a}$, so
$\mathfrak{a}$ is an ideal of $A$.

Suppose to the contrary that $\mathfrak{a}$ is finitely generated. Then, there are finitely many generating elements $x_1,\ldots,x_n$. Each $x_i$ is in some $\mathfrak{b}_i \in \mathcal{T}$.
Since $\mathcal{T}$ is a chain, there is some $j \in \{1,\ldots,n\}$ such that $\mathfrak{b}_i \subset \mathfrak{b}_j$ for all $i$. Then, $x_i \in \mathfrak{b}_i \subset \mathfrak{b}_j$
for all $i$. Since $\mathfrak{b}_j \subset \mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{a} = \sum_i A x_i \subset \sum_i \mathfrak{b}_j = \mathfrak{b}_j$, we have $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}_j$. But
since $\mathfrak{b}_j \in \Sigma$, $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}_j$ is not finitely generated, a contradiction. Thus, $\mathfrak{a} \in \Sigma$, so all chains in $\Sigma$ have maximal
elements in $\Sigma$. By Zorn's lemma, $\Sigma$ has maximal elements.

Now, suppose $\mathfrak{a}$ is a maximal element of $\Sigma$. Suppose there exist $x \not\in \mathfrak{a}$ and $y \not\in \mathfrak{a}$ such that $xy \in \mathfrak{a}$. Thus, the ideal
$\mathfrak{a} + (x)$ is an ideal that properly contains $\mathfrak{a}$. Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is maximal in $\Sigma$, $\mathfrak{a} + (x)$ is finitely generated. In particular, there are
elements $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ that generate $\mathfrak{a} + (x)$ as an $A$-module. Then, for each $x_i$, there is some $a_i \in A$ such that $x_i - a_i x \in \mathfrak{a}$. Let $\mathfrak{a}_0$
be the ideal generated by $\{x_i - a_i x\}_i$, so clearly, $\mathfrak{a}_0 \subset \mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{a}_0$ is finitely generated. Further, $x_i \in \mathfrak{a}_0 + (x)$ for all $i$
since $x_i - a_i x \in \mathfrak{a}_0$ and $a_i x \in (x)$. Therefore, $\mathfrak{a}_0 + (x) = \mathfrak{a} + (x)$.

By the definition of $(\mathfrak{a}:x)$, $x(\mathfrak{a}:x) \subset \mathfrak{a}$. Further, $\mathfrak{a} \subset (\mathfrak{a}:x)$; this inclusion is proper since $y \in (\mathfrak{a}:x)$ but
$y \not\in \mathfrak{a}$. Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is maximal in $\Sigma$, $(\mathfrak{a}:x)$ is finitely generated; hence, $x(\mathfrak{a}:x)$ is finitely generated. Therefore, 
$\mathfrak{a}_0 + x(\mathfrak{a}:x) \subset \mathfrak{a}$. Suppose $a \in \mathfrak{a}$. Then, $a = a' + bx$, where $a' \in \mathfrak{a}_0$ and $b \in A$ 
since $\mathfrak{a}_0 + (x) = \mathfrak{a} + (x)$. Thus, $bx = a - a' \subset \mathfrak{a}$, so $b \in (\mathfrak{a}:x)$. Hence, $bx \in x(\mathfrak{a}:x)$ so that 
$a = a' + bx \in \mathfrak{a}_0 + x(\mathfrak{a}:x)$. Therefore, we have equality: $\mathfrak{a}_0 + x(\mathfrak{a}:x) = \mathfrak{a}$. 

Since $\mathfrak{a}_0$ and $x(\mathfrak{a}:x)$ are both finitely generated, their sum, $\mathfrak{a}$, is also finitely generated. This is a clear contradiction,
so we cannot have $x \not\in \mathfrak{a}$ and $y \not\in \mathfrak{a}$ such that $xy \in \mathfrak{a}$; hence, $\mathfrak{a}$ is prime. Thus, maximal elements of $\Sigma$ is prime.
In particular, if $A$ is a ring in which all prime ideals are finitely generated, then $\Sigma$ must be empty, so every ideal of $A$ is finitely generated; in other words, $A$
is Noetherian.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.2}

\begin{proof}
If $\sum_i a_i x^i \in A[[x]]$ is nilpotent, then by Exercise \ref{1.5}, each $a_i \in A$ is nilpotent. Conversely, suppose $a_i \in A$ is nilpotent for
all $i$. Since $A$ is Noetherian, we have by Corollary 7.15 that the nilradical is nilpotent. Thus, $\mathfrak{N}^m = 0$ for some $m > 0$. Expanding 
$(\sum_i a_i x^i)^m$, we obtain a sum of elements of the form $a_{i_1} \cdots a_{i_m} x^{i_1 + \cdots + i_m}$. Since $a_{i_k} \in \mathfrak{N}$
for all $k$, $a_{i_1} \cdots a_{i_m} \in \mathfrak{N}^m = 0$ so that each term in the sum vanishes. Thus, $(\sum_i a_i x^i)^m = 0$, as desired.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.3}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\implies$ (iii). By assumption, $\mathfrak{a}$ is primary. It is sufficient to show that in the quotient $A/\mathfrak{a}$, $(0:x^n)$ is stationary for all $x \in A/\mathfrak{a}$.
Let $x \in A/\mathfrak{a}$. Suppose $(0:x) = 0$ and let $m > 1$ be minimal such that $(0:x^m) \neq 0$. Then, $x^m y = 0$ for some $y \neq 0$, so $x (x^{m-1} y) = 0$.
Because $(0:x) = 0$, $x^{m-1} y = 0$, so $(0:x^{m-1}) \neq 0$, violating the minimality of $m$. Thus, $(0:x^n) = 0$ for all $n > 0$, so $(0:x^n)$ is clearly stationary. On the other
hand, suppose $(0:x) \neq 0$ so that there is some non-zero $y \in (0:x)$. Then, $x$ is a zero divisor. Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is primary, $x \in A/\mathfrak{a}$ is nilpotent, so there
is some $m > 0$ such that $x^m = 0$. Then, $(0:x^n) = A/\mathfrak{a}$ for all $n \ge m$, so $(0:x^n)$ is stationary.

(iii) $\implies$ (i). By assumption the sequence $(\mathfrak{a}:x^n)$ is stationary for every $x \in A$. Passing to the quotient $A/\mathfrak{a}$, $(0:x^n)$ is stationary
for every $x \in A/\mathfrak{a}$. Suppose $x,y \in A/\mathfrak{a}$ such that $y \neq 0$ and $xy = 0$. Then, $y \in (0:x)$. We have that $(0:x) \subset (0:x^2) \subset \cdots$
is stationary, so $(0:x^n) = (0:x^{n+1}) = \cdots$ for some $n > 0$. If $a \in (y)$, then $ax = 0$, and if $a \in (x^n)$ also, then $a = bx^n$. Hence, $bx^{n+1} = ax = 0$,
so $b \in (0:x^{n+1}) = (0:x^n)$. Thus, $a = bx^n = 0$. In particular, $(y) \cap (x^n) = 0$. Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is irreducible, $0$ is irreducible in $A/\mathfrak{a}$. Since
$y \neq 0$, we must then have $(x^n) = 0$. Thus, every zero divisor $x$ is nilpotent in $A/\mathfrak{a}$, so $\mathfrak{a}$ is primary.

(i) $\implies$ (ii). Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is primary, $\mathfrak{p} = r(\mathfrak{a})$ is prime. Let $S$ be an arbitrary multiplicatively closed set of $A$. If
$S \cap \mathfrak{p} = \emptyset$, then by Proposition 4.8, $(S^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c = \mathfrak{a} = (\mathfrak{a}:1)$. As required, $1 \in S$. On the other hand,
if $S \cap \mathfrak{p} \neq \emptyset$, there is some $x \in S \cap \mathfrak{p}$. Since $\mathfrak{p} = r(\mathfrak{a})$, there is some $n > 0$ such that $x^n \in \mathfrak{a}$. 
Since $S$ is multiplicatively closed and $x \in S$, $x^n \in S \cap \mathfrak{a}$. We then have $(\mathfrak{a}:x^n) = (1)$. By Proposition 4.8, $S^{-1}\mathfrak{a} = S^{-1}A$, 
so $(S^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c = (1) = (\mathfrak{a}:x^n)$, where $x^n \in S$. Thus, for any multiplicatively closed set $S$ of $A$, $(S^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c = (\mathfrak{a}:x)$ for
some $x \in S$.

(ii) $\implies$ (iii). We'll prove the contrapositive, so suppose there is some $x \in A$ such that $(\mathfrak{a}:x^n)$ is not stationary. Let $S_n = \{1,x^n,x^{n+1},\ldots\}$, which
is clearly multiplicatively closed and $S_{n+1} \subset S_n$ for all $n > 0$. Now, suppose to the contrary that for all $n$, we can choose some $s \in S_n$ such that 
$(S_n^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c = (\mathfrak{a}:s)$. If $s = 1$, then $\mathfrak{a} = (\mathfrak{a}:1) = (S_n^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c$. By Proposition 3.11, $S_n$ meets $\mathfrak{a}$,
so for some $k > 0$, $x^k \in \mathfrak{a}$. Thus, for all $m \ge n$, $x^m \in \mathfrak{a}$ so that $(\mathfrak{a}:x^m) = A$, contradicting the fact that $(\mathfrak{a}:x^n)$ is not
stationary. Thus, $s \neq 1$ for any $n > 0$.

Therefore, for all $n > 0$, there is some $s \in S_n$ ($s \neq 1$) such that $(S_n^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c = (\mathfrak{a}:s)$. Thus, there is some $m(n) \ge n$ such that $s = x^{m(n)}$.
Choose $n > 0$. Then, since $S_1 S_n = S_1$, the homomorphism $A \to S_1^{-1}A$ factors through $A \to S_n^{-1}A \to S_1^{-1}A$. Hence, $S_n^{-1}\mathfrak{a} \subset S_1^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$
so that $(S_n^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^c \subset (S_1^{-1} \mathfrak{a})^c$. Thus, $(\mathfrak{a}:x^{m(n)}) \subset (\mathfrak{a}:x^{m(1)})$ so that $m(n) \le m(1)$ since $(\mathfrak{a}:x^n)$ is not
stationary. Then, for all $n$, $n \le m(n) \le m(1)$. This gives us the desired contradiction, as there are $n$ such that $n > m(1)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.4}

(i) The ring of rational functions $\C(z)$ without poles at $z$ for any $\lvert z \rvert = 1$ is a Noetherian ring.

\begin{proof}
By Hilbert's basis theorem, $\C[z]$ is Noetherian. Let $a \in \C$. We have a ring homomorphism $\psi_a : \C[z] \to \C$ via $f \mapsto f(a)$.
This is clearly surjective, so $\C \cong \C[z]/\ker \psi_a$. Since $\ker \psi_a$ is an ideal and $\C$ is a field, $\mathfrak{m}_a = \ker \psi_a$ is a maximal ideal.

Define $S = \C[z] - \bigcup_{a : \lvert a \rvert = 1}\mathfrak{m}_a$. Since $\mathfrak{m}_a$ is prime for all $a$, if $x,y \in S$, $x,y \not\in \mathfrak{m}_a$ for any $a$,
so $xy \not\in \mathfrak{m}_a$ for any $a$, so $xy \in S$. Thus, $S$ is multiplicatively closed.

The ring of rational functions $\C(z)$ without poles at $z$ for any $\lvert z \rvert = 1$ is $S^{-1}\C[z]$. By Proposition 7.3, $S^{-1}\C[z]$ is Noetherian since $\C[z]$ is
Noetherian.
\end{proof}

(ii) The ring of power series of $\C[[z]]$ with a positive radius of convergence, $\C\{z\}$, is Noetherian.

\begin{proof}
First, note that $(z)$ is an ideal of the ring $\C\{z\}$. Now, suppose $f \in \C\{z\} - (z) \subset \C[[z]]$. Then, $f$ has a non-zero constant term, so by Exercise \ref{1.5},
$f$ has an inverse $g$ in $\C[[z]]$. Since $f \in \C\{z\}$, $R_f > 0$ (the radius of convergence of $f$), and since $f(0) \neq 0$, $g(0) \neq 0$ since $fg = 1$. Since $f$ is 
holomorphic on the disk $D(0,R_f)$ and $f(0) \neq 0$, then $1/f$ is holomorphic on $D(0,R_f)$ wherever it does not vanish. Since $f(0) \neq 0$, there is some $\epsilon > 0$
such that $1/f$ is holomorphic on $D(0,\epsilon)$ by the continuity of $f$. Thus, $R_g \ge \epsilon > 0$, so $g \in \C\{z\}$. Thus, for any $f \in \C\{z\} - (z)$, $f$ is a unit.
By Proposition 1.6, $\C\{z\}$ is a local ring and $(z)$ is its maximal ideal.

Now, let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an arbitrary ideal of $\C\{z\}$. Suppose $\mathfrak{a} \neq 0$ and $\mathfrak{a} \neq \C\{z\}$. Then, $\mathfrak{a}$ is contained in some maximal ideal,
so since there is only one, $(z) \supset \mathfrak{a}$. Let $n > 0$ be the minimal $n$ such that $(z)^n \supset \mathfrak{a}$; there is such an $n$ since $(z)^1 \supset \mathfrak{a}$.
Thus, $(z)^n \supset \mathfrak{a}$, but $(z)^{n+1} \not\supset \mathfrak{a}$. Thus, there is some $f \in \mathfrak{a}$ of the form $f = z^n(a_0 + a_1 z + \cdots)$, where $a_0 \neq 0$.
Let $a = a_0 + a_1 z + \cdots$; note that $R_a = R_f > 0$, so $a \in \C\{z\}$. Further, since $a_0 \neq 0$, $a^{-1} \in \C\{z\}$, as we demonstrated above. Since 
$f = a z^n \in \mathfrak{a}$, we have $z^n = a^{-1}f \in \mathfrak{a}$, so $(z^n) = (z)^n \subset \mathfrak{a}$. Thus, $\mathfrak{a} = (z)^n$. In particular, all non-trivial ideals
of $\C\{z\}$ are of the form $(z^n)$ for some $n > 0$.

Every ideal is principal and thus finitely generated, so $\C\{z\}$ is Noetherian.
\end{proof}

(iii) The ring of power series of $\C[[z]]$ with an infinite radius of convergence, $\mathcal{O}(\C)$, is not Noetherian.

\begin{proof}
Every $f \in \mathcal{O}(\C)$ can be evaluating at any point in $\C$. Let $I_n$ be the subset of $\mathcal{O}(\C)$ with zeros at $\{n, n+1, \ldots\}$. Then clearly, these are ideals. 
By the Weierstrass Factorization Theorem, since $\{n,n+1,\ldots\}$ does not have a limit point in $\C$, there exists some $f \in \mathcal{O}(\C)$ with precisely these zeros. 
Thus, the ascending chain of ideals $I_1 \subset I_2 \subset \cdots$ is strictly ascending; hence, $\mathcal{O}(\C)$ is not Noetherian.
\end{proof}

(iv) The ring of polynomials in $z$ whose first $k$ derivatives vanish at the origin, $R_k = \C + z^{k+1}\C[z]$, is Noetherian.

\begin{proof}
Note that $\C$ is Noetherian and that $\C$ is a subring of $R_k$. In fact, $R_k$ is generated as a $\C$ algebra by $z^{k+1},\ldots,z^{2k+1}$, so $R_k$ is a finitely generated 
$\C$-algebra. By Proposition 7.2, $R_k$ is Noetherian.
\end{proof}

(v) The ring of polynomials in $\C[w,z]$ whose partial derivatives with respect to $w$ vanish for $z = 0$, $R = \C + z\C[w,z]$, is not Noetherian.

\begin{proof}
Let $I_n = (z, wz, \ldots, w^n z)$ for each $n > 0$. Suppose to the contrary that $w^{n+1}z \in I_n$. Then, $w^{n+1}z = f_0 z + \cdots f_n w^n z$, where $f_i \in \C + z\C[w,z]$. Then,
since $\C[w,z]$ is an integral domain, we have (in $\C[w,z]$) $w^{n+1} = f_0 + \cdots + f_n w^n$. Mapping this relation $\C[w,z] \to \C[w]$ via $z \mapsto 0$, we have
$w^{n+1} = \overline{f_0} + \cdots + \overline{f_n} w^n$. Since $f_i \in \C + z\C[w,z]$, under the map $z \mapsto 0$, $\overline{f_i} \in \C$. Thus, 
$w^{n+1} = \overline{f_0} + \cdots + \overline{f_n} w^n$ contradicts the fact that $w$ is transcendental in $\C[w]$. Hence, the inclusion $I_n \subset I_{n+1}$ is proper for all $n > 0$.
Therefore, the chain of ideals $I_1 \subset I_2 \subset \cdots$ is strictly ascending, so $R$ is not Noetherian.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.6}

\begin{proof}
Let $K$ be a finitely generated ring that is also a field. If $K$ has characteristic $0$, we have $\Z \subset \Q \subset K$. Since $K$ is finitely generated over $\Z$,
it is finitely generated over $\Q$. Then, by Proposition 7.9, $K$ is a finite algebraic extension of $\Q$, so $K$ is finitely generated as a $\Q$-module. Since $\Z$ is
Noetherian, we have by Proposition 7.8 that $\Q$ is a finitely generated $\Z$-algebra. Let $p_i/q_i \in \Q$ be the finite generators of $\Q$ as a $\Z$-algebra. Since there
are finitely many $\{q_i\}$, finitely many primes divide each, and there are infinitely many primes of $\Z$, there is a prime $p$ of $\Z$ that does not divide any $q_i$.
In particular, $p_i/q_i \in \Z_{(p)}$ for all $i$. Since $\Z_{(p)}$ is a $\Z$-algebra that contains each generator of $\Q$ as a $\Z$-algebra, we must have
$\Q \subset \Z_{(p)}$; this is a clear contradiction as $1/p \in \Q$ but $1/p \not\in \Z_{(p)}$.

Thus, $K$ has characteristic $p > 0$, so it is finitely generated as a $\Z/(p)$-algebra. Since $\Z/(p)$ is a field, we have by Proposition 7.9 that $K$ is a finite algebraic
extension of $\Z/(p)$. Thus, $K$ is a finitely generated $\Z/(p)$-module. In particular, for some $n > 0$, we have a surjective $\Z/(p)$-module homomorphism $(\Z/(p))^n \to K$.
Since $(\Z/(p))^n$ has $p^n$ elements, $K$ has at most $p^n$ elements, so $K$ is a finite field.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.7}

\begin{proof}
Let $X$ be an affine algebraic variety given by a family of equations $f_{\alpha}(t_1,\ldots,t_n) = 0$; this defines an ideal $I(X)$ of $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$. 
$k$ is a field and thus Noetherian, so $k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]$ is Noetherian by Corollary 7.6. Therefore, $I(X)$ is a finitely
generated ideal, so let $I_0$ be a finite set of generators. Let $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in X$. Then, $f_{\beta}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = 0$ for all $f_{\beta} \in I_0$,
so $x \in V(I_0)$. Conversely, suppose $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in V(I_0)$ so that $f_{\beta}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = 0$ for all $f_{\beta} \in I_0$. 
Then, since each $f_{\alpha} \in I(X)$ is generated by elements $f_{\beta} \in I_0$, $f_{\alpha}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = 0$. Thus, $X = V(I_0)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.9}

\begin{proof}
The ideal $0$ is clearly finitely generated, so let $\mathfrak{a} \neq 0$ be an ideal in $A$. Then, there is some non-zero $x_0 \in \mathfrak{a}$.
By property (2), there are finitely many maximal ideals that contain $x_0$. Thus, there are finitely many maximal ideals that contain $\mathfrak{a}$.
Order them such that $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_r$ are the maximal ideals containing $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{r+s}$
are the maximal ideals containing $x_0$.

Since $\mathfrak{m}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{r+s}$ do not contain $\mathfrak{a}$, there exist $x_j \in \mathfrak{a} - \mathfrak{m}_{r+j}$ for each $j$ 
($1 \le j \le s$). By property (1), $A_{\mathfrak{m}_i}$ is Noetherian for all $i$, so the extension of $\mathfrak{a}$ to $A_{\mathfrak{m}_i}$ is finitely
generated. Pulling back these finitely many sets of finitely many generating elements, we have elements $x_{s+1},\ldots,x_t$ in $\mathfrak{a}$ whose image in
each $A_{\mathfrak{m}_i}$ generate $A_{\mathfrak{m}_i}\mathfrak{a}$ for all $i$ ($1 \le i \le r$). 
Let $\mathfrak{a}_0 = (x_0,\ldots,x_t)$. Since $x_i \in \mathfrak{a}$ for all $i$ ($0 \le i \le t$), $\mathfrak{a}_0 \subset \mathfrak{a}$. 

Now, let $\mathfrak{m}$ be any maximal ideal of $A$. If $\mathfrak{m}$ is one of $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_r$, then $A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}$ 
is generated by $x_{s+1},\ldots,x_t \in A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}_0$, so $A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a} = A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}_0$. If $\mathfrak{m}$
is one of $\mathfrak{m}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{r+s}$, then $x_j \in A - \mathfrak{m}_{r+j}$, so by Proposition 3.11, an ideal of $A$ that contains $x_j$
extends to $(1)$ in $A_{\mathfrak{m}_{r+j}}$. But $x_j \in \mathfrak{a}_0 \subset \mathfrak{a}$, so $A_{\mathfrak{m}_{r+j}}\mathfrak{a} = A_{\mathfrak{m}_{r+j}} 
= A_{\mathfrak{m}_{r+j}}\mathfrak{a}_0$. Finally, suppose $\mathfrak{m}$ is not any of $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{r+s}$. Then, $x_0 \in A - \mathfrak{m}$.
Since $x_0 \in \mathfrak{a}_0 \subset \mathfrak{a}$, we have by Proposition 3.11 that $A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a} = A_{\mathfrak{m}} = A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}_0$.
Thus, for all maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$, $A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a} = A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}_0$.

The inclusion $\mathfrak{a}_0 \xhookrightarrow{} \mathfrak{a}$ is an $A$-module homomorphism. For all maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$, the localization
of this map, $A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}_0 \to A_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{a}$, is surjective. Thus, by Proposition 3.9, the original map is surjective,
so $\mathfrak{a}_0 = \mathfrak{a}$. Therefore, every ideal of $A$ is finitely generated, so $A$ is Noetherian.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.10}

\begin{proof}
Let $N \neq 0$ be an $A[x]$-submodule of $M[x]$.
Define $N_i$ to be the set of elements $m \in M$ that occur as the coefficient of the leading term of a polynomial of degree $i$ in $N$,
including $0$. In particular, if $m \in N_i$, there is some polynomial $p(x) \in N$ with leading term $m x^i$. Since $N$ is an $A[x]$-module,
$x p(x) \in N$, which has leading term $m x^{i+1}$; thus, $m \in N_{i+1}$. Then, $N_i \subset N_{i+1}$ for all $i$, giving us an ascending
chain of $A$-submodules of $M$. Since $M$ is a Noetherian $A$-module, this chain becomes stationary for some $n \ge 0$.

Since $M$ is a Noetherian $A$-module, each $A$-submodule $N_i$ is finitely generated. Thus, for each $i$, we have a finite collection of 
$A$-module generators $\{m_{i,1},\ldots,m_{i,k_i}\} \subset N_i$. Then, by the definition of $N_i$, we can associate to each $m_{i,j}$
an element $f_{i,j} \in N$ such that $f_{i,j}$ has leading term $m_{i,j} x^i$. Let $N'$ be the $A[x]$-submodule generated by 
$\{f_{i,j}\}$, where $0 \le i \le n$ and $1 \le j \le k_i$. Then clearly, $N' \subset N$ and $N'$ is finitely generated.

We prove that $N \subset N'$ by induction on degree. If $f \in N$ has degree $0$, then $f = m \in M$, so $f = m \in N_0$. Since we're in 
degree $0$, $m_{0,j} = f_{0,j}$ for all $1 \le j \le k_0$. Thus, $N_0 \subset N'$ so that $f \in N'$. Now, suppose that all polynomials 
$f \in N$ of degree less than $r$ are in $N'$. If $f \in N$ has degree $r$, then $f$ has leading term $m x^r$, where $m \in N_r$ by definition.

First, suppose $r \le n$. Then, $m = \sum_{j=1}^{k_r} a_j m_{r,j}$ since $\{m_{r,j}\}$ generate $N_r$, so the element 
$p(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k_r} a_j f_{r,j} \in N' \subset N$ has leading term $m x^r$. Thus, $f - p(x) \in N$ is $0$ or has degree less than $r$. Using the
inductive hypothesis, $f - p(x) \in N'$. Thus, $f = p(x) + (f - p(x)) \in N'$, as desired. Now, suppose $r > n$. Then, $N_r = N_n$, so $m \in N_n$.
Thus, $m = \sum_{j=1}^{k_n} a_j m_{n,j}$ so that $p(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k_n} a_j f_{n,j} \in N' \subset N$ has leading term $m x^n$. Since $N'$ is an
$A[x]$-module, $x^{r - n}p(x) \in N' \subset N$ and has leading term $mx^r$. Then, $f - x^{r - n}p(x) \in N$ is $0$ or has degree less than $r$,
so using the inductive hypothesis, $f - x^{r - n}p(x) \in N'$, so $f = x^{r - n}p(x) + (f - x^{r - n}p(x)) \in N'$, as desired. By induction,
$N = N'$. Hence, every $A[x]$-submodule $N$ of $M[x]$ is finitely generated, so $M[x]$ is a Noetherian $A[x]$-module by Proposition 6.2.
\end{proof}

Suppose $E$ is a finite union of sets of the form $U \cap C$, where $U$ is open and $C$ is closed. Then, $C^c$ is open, so $U$ and $C^c$ are in $\mathcal{F}$. Thus, $C \in \mathcal{F}$,

since $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under finite intersections and complements; thus, $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under finite unions as well. Since $E$ is a finite union of sets of the form 
$U \cap C$ ($\in \mathcal{F}$), we have $E \in \mathcal{F}$.

Conversely, note that $\mathcal{F}$ is generated by the open sets of $X$ under finite intersections and complements. Every open set is trivially a finite union of sets of the form $U \cap C$,
where $U$ is open and $C$ is closed since an open set $U$ is $U \cap X$ (noting that $X$ is closed). Thus, it suffices to show that sets of this form are closed under complements and finite 
intersections. Suppose we have two sets of this form: $\bigcup_i U_i \cap C_i$ and $\bigcup_j V_j \cap K_j$. Then,
$$\left(\bigcup_i U_i \cap C_i\right)\cap\left(\bigcup_j V_j \cap K_j\right) = \bigcup_{(i,j)} (U_i \cap C_i) \cap (V_j \cap K_j) = \bigcup_{(i,j)} (U_i \cap V_j) \cap (C_i \cap K_j).$$
Since $U_i \cap V_j$ is open and $C_i \cap K_j$ is closed, the intersection has the desired form. Moving to complements, we have
$$\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i\right)^c = \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i^c \cup C_i^c = \bigcup_{A \in \mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})} \left(\bigcap_{i \in A} C_i^c \cap \bigcap_{i \not\in A} U_i^c\right).$$
This is a finite union, $\bigcap C_i^c$ is open (even if $X$, i.e. empty indexing set), and $\bigcap U_i^c$ is closed (even if $X$, i.e. empty indexing set). Thus, complements preserve the form.
Hence, a set $E$ of $X$ is in $\mathcal{F}$ if and only if it is a finite union of sets of the form $U \cap C$, where $U$ is open and $C$ is closed.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(ii) Proof.]
Suppose $E$ contains a non-empty open set $U$ of $X$. Since $X$ is irreducible, $X = \overline{U} \subset \overline{E} \subset X$, so $E$ is dense in $X$.

Conversely, suppose $E \in \mathcal{F}$ is dense in $X$. By (i), $E = \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i$, where each $U_i$ is open and each $C_i$ is closed. If $U_i \cap C_i$
is empty, we may exclude it from the union; thus, we can assume $U_i \cap C_i$ is non-empty for all $i$. Then,
$E \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^n C_i$, a closed set, so $X = \overline{E} \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^n C_i \subset X$, giving equality throughout. Taking complements,
$\emptyset = \bigcap_{i=1}^n C_i^c$. Since $X$ is irreducible and $\bigcap_{i=1}^n C_i^c$ is an empty intersection of finitely many open sets, there is some $i$
such that $C_i^c$ is empty. Thus, $C_i = X$ so that $E \supset U_i \cap C_i = U_i \cap X = U_i$. Since $U_i \cap C_i$ is non-empty and equal to $U_i$, $U_i$ is 
a non-empty open subset of $E$, as desired.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.21}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $E \in \mathcal{F}$, and let $X_0$ be an irreducible closed set of $X$. By Exercise \ref{7.20}, $E = \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i$, where the $U_i$ are open and
the $C_i$ are closed. Let $E_0 = E \cap X_0$, and suppose $\overline{E_0} = \overline{E \cap X_0} = X_0$. Since $X_0$ is an irreducible closed set of $X$, $X_0$ is an
irreducible topological space. Let $\mathcal{F}_0$ be the collection of $X_0$ defined in Exercise \ref{7.20}. Note that 
$$E_0 = X_0 \cap \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i = \bigcup_{i=1}^n X_0 \cap (U_i \cap C_i) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n (X_0 \cap U_i) \cap (X_0 \cap C_i).$$
Since $X_0 \cap U_i$ are open in $X_0$ and $X_0 \cap C_i$ are closed in $X_0$, both by definition, we see by Exercise \ref{7.20} (i) that $E_0 \in \mathcal{F}_0$. Then,
since $X_0$ is irreducible and $E_0$ is dense in $X_0$, we have by Exercise \ref{7.20} (ii) that $E_0$ contains a non-empty open subset of $X_0$. Therefore, we've shown
that if $E \in \mathcal{F}$, then $\overline{E \cap X_0} \neq X_0$ or $E \cap X_0$ contains a non-empty open subset of $X_0$.

Conversely, suppose $E \not\in \mathcal{F}$. Then, the collection of closed sets $X' \subset X$ such that $E \cap X' \not\in \mathcal{F}$ is not empty ($X$ is such a closed set). 
This collection has a minimal element $X_0$ since $X$ is a Noetherian space. Suppose $X_0$ is reducible, i.e. there are two closed sets $C_1$ and $C_2$ such that $X_0 = C_1 \cup C_2$
and $X_0$ properly contains each closed set. Since $X_0$ is minimal in the collection, $E\cap C_1, E\cap C_2 \in \mathcal{F}$. Since $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under finite unions (as we 
saw in the proof of Exercise \ref{7.20} (i)), $E \cap X_0 = (E \cap C_1) \cup (E \cap C_2) \in \mathcal{F}$, a contradiction. Thus, $X_0$ is closed and irreducible.

Suppose to the contrary that $\overline{E \cap X_0} \neq X_0$. Then, $X_0$ properly contains the closed set $\overline{E \cap X_0}$, so $E \cap \overline{E \cap X_0} \in \mathcal{F}$
by the minimality of $X_0$ in the aforementioned collection. Since $X_0$ is a closed set, $X_0 \in \mathcal{F}$, so we have 
$$X_0 \cap (E \cap \overline{E \cap X_0}) = (E \cap X_0) \cap \overline{E \cap X_0} = E \cap X_0 \in \mathcal{F}.$$
This contradicts the definition of $X_0$.

Now, suppose to the contrary that $E \cap X_0$ contains a non-empty open set $X_0 \cap U$. We can thus write 
$$E \cap X_0 = (E \cap X_0 \cap U) \cup (E \cap X_0 \cap U^c).$$
Since $X_0 \cap U^c$ is closed and properly contained in $X_0$ (since $X_0 \cap U$ is non-empty), by the minimality of $X_0$ in the collection, 
$E \cap (X_0 \cap U^c) \in \mathcal{F}$. On the other hand, since $E \cap X_0$
contains $U \cap X_0$, $E \cap X_0 \cap U = X_0 \cap U$. Since $X_0$ is closed and $U$ is open, we have by Exercise \ref{7.20} (i) that $X_0 \cap U \in \mathcal{F}$.
Thus, $E \cap X_0$ is the union of two sets in $\mathcal{F}$, so $E \cap X_0 \in \mathcal{F}$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The sets belonging to $\mathcal{F}$ are called the ``constructible'' subsets of $X$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $E$ is an open set of $X$. Let $X_0$ be an irreducible closed subset of $X$. If $E \cap X_0 \neq \emptyset$, then $E \cap X_0$ is
a non-empty open subset of $X_0$. Thus, if $E$ is an open set of $X$, then for every irreducible closed subset $X_0$ of $X$,

such that $E \cap C$ is not open in $C$ is non-empty. Since $X$ is Noetherian, this collection has a minimal element $X_0$. Suppose that $X_0$ is 
reducible so that $X_0 = C_1 \cup C_2$ and $X_0$ properly contains both closed sets $C_1$ and $C_2$. Then, by minimality, $E \cap C_1$ is open in 
$C_1$ and $E \cap C_2$ is open in $C_2$. Thus, $E \cap C_i = V_i \cap C_i$ for open sets $V_1$, $V_2$ of $X$. Let $U_i = V_i \cup C_i^c$, so $U_i$

$$E \cap X_0 = (E \cap C_1) \cup (E \cap C_2) = (U \cap C_1) \cup (U \cap C_2) = U \cap (C_1 \cup C_2) = U \cap X_0,$$

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.23}

\begin{proof}
By Exercise \ref{7.20}, a constructible set has the form $\bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i$, where $U_i$ are open sets and $C_i$ are closed. Then,
$f^*\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i \cap C_i\right) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n f^*(U_i \cap C_i)$. Since constructible sets are closed under finite unions, it is
enough to show that $f^*(U \cap C)$ is constructible for every open set $U$ and closed set $C$.

Since $C$ is closed in $\Spec(B)$, we have $C = V(\mathfrak{b})$ for some ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ of $B$. Then, we can extend our homomorphism $f$ to
$\overline{f} : A \to B \to B/\mathfrak{b}$. By Exercise \ref{1.21}, $V(\mathfrak{b})$ is homeomorphic to $\Spec(B/\mathfrak{b})$ so that 
$\overline{f}^* : \Spec(B/\mathfrak{b}) \cong V(\mathfrak{b}) \xhookrightarrow{} \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)$. Since 
$U \cap C = U \cap V(\mathfrak{b}) \subset V(\mathfrak{b})$, $f^*(U \cap C) = \overline{f}^*(U \cap V(\mathfrak{b}))$. But $U \cap V(\mathfrak{b})$
is open in the subspace $V(\mathfrak{b})$, by definition. Since $f$ is a homomorphism of finite type, $\overline{f} : A \to B/\mathfrak{b}$ is a 
homomorphism of finite type. Therefore, if we can show that for all homomorphisms $f : A \to B$ of finite type, $f^*$ maps open sets to constructible
sets, we'd be done.

We've reduced the problem to showing that for $A$ a Noetherian ring and $f : A \to B$ a homomorphism of finite type, $f^*$ maps open sets to constructible
sets. Let $U$ be an open set of $\Spec(B)$. First of all, since $f$ is a homomorphism of finite type, $B$ is also Noetherian (Corollary 7.7). Thus, by
Exercise \ref{6.8}, $\Spec(B)$ is a Noetherian topological space. Hence, by Exercise \ref{6.6}, $U$ is quasi-compact in $\Spec(B)$. Since the open sets 
$V(g)^c$ ($g \in B$) form a basis of open sets of $\Spec(B)$ (Exercise \ref{1.17}), $U$ is the union of sets of this form. But $U$ is quasi-compact, so
it is the union of finitely many open sets of the form $V(g)^c$. Thus, we write $U = \bigcup_{i=1}^n V(g_i)^c$. Let $f_i : B \to B_{g_i}$ be the canonical
homomorphism. By Exercise \ref{3.21}, $f_i^* : \Spec(B_{g_i}) \to V(g_i)^c$ is a homeomorphism. Since $f : A \to B$ is of finite type and $f_i : B \to B_{g_i}$
is of finite type, $f_i \circ f : A \to B_g$ is of finite type. Suppose that we have shown that for all homomorphisms $\psi : A \to B'$ of finite type,
$\psi*(\Spec(B'))$ is a constructible set. Then, 
$$f^*(U) = f^*\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n V(g_i)^c\right) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n f^*(f_i^*(\Spec(B_{g_i}))) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n (f_i \circ f)^*(\Spec(B_{g_i}))$$
is constructible since it is a finite union of constructible sets.

Hence, we have reduced the problem to showing that for $A$ a Noetherian ring and $f : A \to B$ a homomorphism of finite type, $f^*(\Spec(B))$ is a constructible
set. Since $A$ is Noetherian, $\Spec(A)$ is a Noetherian topological space (Exercise \ref{6.8}). Thus, to show that $f^*(\Spec(B))$, we can use the criterion
of Exercise \ref{7.21}: for each irreducible closed set $X_0 \subset \Spec(A)$, we must show that $\overline{f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0} \neq X_0$ or else
$f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0$ contains a non-empty open subset of $X_0$. If $\overline{f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0} \neq X_0$ for all $X_0$, we're done, so suppose we 
have an irreducible closed subspace $X_0$ such that $\overline{f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0} = X_0$. 

We'll show now that $f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0 = f^*(f^{*-1}(X_0))$. If $\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0$, then there is some $\mathfrak{q} \in \Spec(B)$ such that
$\mathfrak{p} = f^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}) \in X_0$. Then, $\mathfrak{q} \in f^{*-1}(X_0)$ since $\mathfrak{p} \in X_0$ so that $\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(f^{*-1}(X_0))$. Conversely,
if $\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(f^{*-1}(X_0))$, then clearly, $\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(\Spec(B))$. Further, there is some $\mathfrak{q} \in f^{*-1}(X_0)$ such that 
$f^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}) = \mathfrak{p}$. But since $f^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}) \in X_0$, $\mathfrak{p} \in X_0$, so $\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap X_0$. Thus, we 
need to show that $f^*(f^{*-1}(X_0))$ contains a non-empty open subset of $X_0$.

Since $X_0$ is a closed, $X_0 = V(\mathfrak{a})$ for some radical ideal $\mathfrak{a}$. Since $A$ is Noetherian, $A/\mathfrak{a}$ is Noetherian (Proposition 7.1).
Thus, by Exercise \ref{6.9}, $A/\mathfrak{a}$ has finitely many minimal primes. Since $\mathfrak{a}$ is a radical ideal, it is the intersection of prime ideals
containing it. This intersection is clearly identical to the intersection over the subset of minimal elements, which, as we just saw, is finite. Thus, $\mathfrak{a}$
is a finite intersection of the prime ideals minimal over $\mathfrak{a}$: $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n$. If there are no primes, 
then $\mathfrak{a} = A$ so that $X_0 = V(A) = \emptyset$, a violation of irreducibility. Thus, $n \ge 1$. Suppose $n \ge 2$. By Exercise \ref{1.15},
$V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(\mathfrak{p}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n) = V(\mathfrak{p}_1) \cup V(\mathfrak{p}_2 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n)$. Since $V(\mathfrak{a})$
is irreducible, we must have $V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(\mathfrak{p}_1)$ or $V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(\mathfrak{p}_2 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n)$. In the first case, we have
$\mathfrak{p}_2 \in V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(\mathfrak{p}_1)$, so $\mathfrak{p}_2 \supset \mathfrak{p}_1$, violating the fact that $\mathfrak{p}_2$ is a minimal prime
over $\mathfrak{a}$. In the second case, $\mathfrak{p}_1 \in V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(\mathfrak{p}_2 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n)$, so 
$\mathfrak{p}_1 \supset \mathfrak{p}_2 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n$. By Proposition 1.11, $\mathfrak{p}_1 \supset \mathfrak{p}_i$ for some $i = 2,\ldots,n$.
But this violates the fact that $\mathfrak{p}_1$ is a minimal prime over $\mathfrak{a}$. Thus, we have a contradiction, so $n=1$. In other words, $X_0 = V(\mathfrak{p})$
for some $\mathfrak{p} \in \Spec(A)$.

Returning to the central task, we must show that $f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap V(\mathfrak{p}) = f^*(f^{*-1}(V(\mathfrak{p})))$ contains a non-empty open subset of $V(\mathfrak{p})$.
By Exercise \ref{1.21}, $f^*{-1}(V(\mathfrak{p})) = V(\mathfrak{p}^e)$, so by Exercise \ref{3.21}, $f^*$ restricts to a map
$\overline{f}^* : \Spec(B/\mathfrak{p}^e) \to \Spec(A/\mathfrak{p})$. Then, $f^*(f^{*-1}(V(\mathfrak{p}))) = f^*(V(\mathfrak{p}^e)) = \overline{f}^*(\Spec(B/\mathfrak{p}^e))$.
Thus, we want to show that $\overline{f}^*(\Spec(B/\mathfrak{p}^e))$ contains a non-empty open subset of $\Spec(A/\mathfrak{p})$. Since $f : A \to B$ is of finite type
and $A$ and $B$ are Noetherian, $\overline{f} : A/\mathfrak{p} \to B/\mathfrak{p}^e$ is of finite type, and $A/\mathfrak{p}$ and $B/\mathfrak{p}^e$ are Noetherian
(Proposition 7.1). Note that $A/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain. Finally, recall that we assumed $V(\mathfrak{p}) = \overline{f^*(\Spec(B)) \cap V(\mathfrak{p})}
= \overline{\overline{f}^*(\Spec(B/\mathfrak{p}^e))}$, and note that $\Spec(A/\mathfrak{p})$ is irreducible by the irreducibility of $V(\mathfrak{p})$.

Thus, we've reduced the problem again. For a homomorphism $f : A \to B$ of finite type, where $A$ is a Noetherian integral domain, $\Spec(A)$ is irreducible, and 
$\overline{f^*(\Spec(B))} = \Spec(A)$, we must show that $f^*(\Spec(B))$ contains a non-empty open subset of $\Spec(A)$. Since $A$ is Noetherian, $B$ is Noetherian 
(Corollary 7.7), so by Exercise \ref{6.8}, $\Spec(B)$ is a Noetherian space. By Exercise \ref{6.7}, $\Spec(B)$ is a finite union of irreducible closed subspaces 
$Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$. Thus, it is sufficient to show that for some $i$, $f^*(Y_i)$ contains a non-empty open set of $\Spec(A)$. We have
$$\Spec(A) = \overline{f^*(\Spec(B))} = \bigcup_{i=1}^n \overline{f^*(Y_i)}.$$
Since $\Spec(A)$ is irreducible, we must have $\overline{f^*(Y)} = \Spec(A)$ for some $Y = Y_i$. As we saw earlier, an irreducible closed subspace $Y$ must have the
form $Y = V(\mathfrak{q})$ for some $\mathfrak{q} \in \Spec(B)$, so we have $\Spec(A) = \overline{f^*(V(\mathfrak{q}))}$. By Exercise \ref{1.21}, $V(\mathfrak{q})$
is homeomorphic to $\Spec(B/\mathfrak{q})$ so that under the composition $g : A \to B \to B/\mathfrak{q}$, 
$\overline{g^*(\Spec(B/\mathfrak{q}))} = \overline{f^*(V(\mathfrak{q}))} = \Spec(A)$. By Exercise \ref{1.21}, since $g^*(\Spec(B/\mathfrak{q}))$ is dense in $\Spec(A)$,
$\ker g \subset \mathfrak{N}_A = 0$, so $g$ is injective. Note that $B/\mathfrak{q}$ is an integral domain, and note that $g$ is of finite type since it is a composition 
of two homomorphisms of finite type.

We've reduced the problem one last time: $f : A \to B$ is an injective homomorphism of finite type, where $A$ and $B$ are integral domains, and we want to show that
$f^*(\Spec(B))$ contains a non-empty open subset of $\Spec(A)$. We're now in the situation of Exercise \ref{5.21}, so there exists a non-zero $s \in A$ such that
given an algebraically closed field $\Omega$ and homomorphism $\phi : A \to \Omega$ (where $\phi(s) \neq 0$), we can extend $\phi$ to a homomorphism $B \to \Omega$.
The image of both homomorphisms are subrings of a field, hence integral domains, so the kernels $\mathfrak{p}$ and $\mathfrak{q}$ are prime with 
$\mathfrak{p} = f^*(\mathfrak{q})$. We have $\phi(s) \neq 0$ if and only if $s \not\in \ker \phi = \mathfrak{p}$, so for all $\mathfrak{p} \in V(s)^c$, we have
$\mathfrak{p} \in f^*(\Spec(B))$ so that $V(s)^c$ is an open subset of $f^*(\Spec(B))$. Since $A$ is an integral domain, $s$ is not nilpotent. Thus, by $V(s)^c$
is non-empty by Exercise \ref{1.17}, as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is ``Chevalley's Theorem'': If $A$ is a Noetherian ring and $f : A \to B$ is a homomorphism of finite type, $f^*$ sends constructible sets to constructible sets.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[(i) Proof.]
Let $\lambda$ be an additive function on the class of finitely generated $A$-modules with values in an abelian group $G$. If a homomorphism 
$\lambda_0 : K(A) \to G$ exists such that $\lambda(M) = \lambda_0(\gamma(M))$ for all $M$, then since the elements $\gamma(M)$ generate $K(A)$, this map is clearly unique. 
Thus, we must construct $\lambda_0$. Let $C$ be the free abelian group of isomorphism classes of finitely generated $A$-modules, and let $n_1(M_1) + \cdots + n_t(M_t)$
be an arbitrary element of $C$. Then, we define $\lambda' : C \to G$ by $\lambda'(n_1(M_1) + \cdots + n_t(M_t)) = n_1\lambda(M_1) + \cdots + n_t\lambda(M_t)$.
Clearly, $\lambda'$ is a homomorphism. Let $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ be an arbitrary short exact sequence. Since $\lambda$ is an additive function,
$\lambda(M') - \lambda(M) + \lambda(M'') = 0$. Thus, $\lambda'((M') - (M) + (M'')) = 0$. Therefore, if $D$ is the subgroup of $C$ generated by these elements,
$\lambda'(D) = 0$. Hence, $\lambda'$ lifts to a well-defined homomorphism $\lambda_0 : K(A) \to G$ since $K(A) = C/D$. Then, $\lambda_0(\gamma(M)) = \lambda'(M) = \lambda(M)$,
as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(ii) Proof.]
Since $A$ is Noetherian, if $M$ is a finitely-generated $A$-module, we have by Exercise \ref{7.18} that there exists a chain of submodules
$$0 = M_0 \subset M_1 \subset \cdots \subset M_r = M$$
such that $M_i/M_{i-1} \cong A/\mathfrak{p}_i$ for prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_i$ of $A$. For each $i$ then, we have a short exact sequence
$$0 \to M_{i-1} \to M_i \to A/\mathfrak{p}_i \to 0$$
so that $\gamma(M_{i-1}) - \gamma(M_i) + \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_i) = 0$, or rather, $\gamma(M_i) = \gamma(M_{i-1}) + \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_i)$. For $i=1$, we have 
$\gamma(M_1) = \gamma(M_0) + \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_1) = \gamma(0) + \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_1) = \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_1)$. By a simple inductive argument, we see that
$$\gamma(M) = \gamma(M_r) = \gamma(A/\matfrak{p}_r) + \cdots + \gamma(A/\mathfrak{p}_1).$$
Thus, $K(A)$ is generated by elements of the form $\gamma(A/\mathfrak{p})$ for $\mathfrak{p} \in \Spec(A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(iii) Proof.]
Let $A$ be a principal ideal domain. Using the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID, every finitely generated $A$-module $M$ can be decomposed as
$$M \cong A^n \oplus A/(a_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus A/(a_k)$$
for elements $a_i \in A$. Thus, $\gamma(M) = n\gamma(A) + \gamma(A/(a_1)) + \cdots + \gamma(A/(a_k))$. Now, let $a \in A$. Then, we have a short exact sequence
$$0 \to A \xrightarrow{\cdot a} A \to A/(a) \to 0.$$
Thus, $\gamma(A) - \gamma(A) + \gamma(A/(a)) = 0$, so $\gamma(A/(a)) = 0$ for every $a \in A$. Hence, $\gamma(M) = n\gamma(A)$. Finally then, $K(A)$ is a free abelian group 
with one generator, $\gamma(A)$, so $K(A) \cong \Z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(iv) Proof.]
Let $f : A \to B$ be a finite homomorphism. By restriction of scalars, $B$-modules are $A$-modules. In fact, since $f$ is finite, finitely generated $B$-modules are 
finitely generated $A$-modules, so if $C(R)$ is the free abelian group of isomorphism classes of $R$-modules, we have a homomorphism $C(B) \to C(A)$. Thus, we have a 
homomorphism $C(B) \to K(A)$. If we have a short exact sequence of $B$-modules, $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$, this is a short exact sequence of $A$-modules, as the maps
are the same. Thus, $D(B) \subset \ker (C(B) \to K(A))$, so the homomorphism $C(B) \to K(A)$ lifts to a homomorphism $f_! : K(B) \to K(A)$.

Now, suppose we have another finite homomorphism $B \to C$. To show that $(g \circ f)_! = f_! \circ g_!$, first note the commutativity of the following diagram
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
C(C) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & C(B) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & C(A) \arrow[d] \\
K(C) \arrow[r, "g_!"] & K(B) \arrow[r, "f_!"] & K(A).
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Then, it is sufficient to see that the composition $C(C) \to C(B) \to C(A)$ is the map produced by $g \circ f$. This is clear; if $M$ is a $C$-module, then it is a $B$-module
via $g$, so it is an $A$-module via $f$. The $A$-module structure of $M$ is thus produced by the map $g \circ f$, as desired.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exercise}\label{7.27}

\begin{proof}[(i) Proof.]
By Exercise \ref{2.8}, If $M$ and $N$ are flat, finitely generated $A$-modules, then $M \otimes_A N$ is a flat $A$-module. Also, since $M$ and $N$ are finitely generated 
by elements $\{m_i\}$ and $\{n_j\}$, $M \otimes_A N$ is generated as an $A$-module by elements $m_i \otimes n_j$, so $M \otimes_A N$ is also finitely generated. Thus,
for $\gamma_1(M),\gamma_1(N) \in K_1(A)$, $\gamma_1(M \otimes N) \in K_1(A)$. Thus, define $\gamma_1(M) \cdot \gamma_1(N) = \gamma_1(M \otimes N)$. The commutativity of
the product is inherited from the commutativity of the tensor product.

For all $\gamma_1(M) \in K_1(A)$, we have $\gamma_1(A) \cdot \gamma_1(M) = \gamma_1(A \otimes M) = \gamma_1(M)$ since $A \otimes_A M \cong M$ as $A$-modules. Thus, $\gamma_1(A)$
is the multiplicative identity. Since $K_1(A)$ is already an abelian additive group, to see that $K_1(A)$ has the structure of a commutative ring, the only thing left to show is
the distributive property. Let $M,N,P$ be flat, finitely generated $A$-modules. Then,
$$\gamma_1(P) \cdot (\gamma_1(M) + \gamma_1(N)) = \gamma_1(P) \cdot \gamma_1(M \oplus N)$$ 
$$= \gamma_1(P \otimes (M \oplus N)) = \gamma_1((P \otimes M) \oplus (P \otimes N))$$ 
$$= \gamma_1(P \otimes M) + \gamma_1(P \otimes N) = \gamma_1(P)\cdot \gamma_1(M) + \gamma_1(P) \cdot \gamma_1(N),$$
as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(ii) Proof.]
We wish to show that $K(A)$ has a $K_1(A)$-module structure defined by $\gamma_1(M) \cdot \gamma(N) = \gamma(M \otimes N)$. Since $M$ and $N$ are both finitely generated $A$-modules,
$M \otimes N$ is a finitely generated $A$-module, so $\gamma(M \otimes N) \in K(A)$, as required.

With $1 = \gamma_1(A) \in K_1(A)$, we have
$$1 \cdot \gamma(N) = \gamma_1(A) \cdot \gamma(N) = \gamma(A \otimes N) = \gamma(N),$$
so the identity acts appropriately. Now, we show that the action is compatible with the additive structure of $K(A)$.
Let $P$ be flat, finitely generated $A$-module, and let $M,N$ be finitely generated $A$-modules. Then,
$$\gamma_1(P) \cdot (\gamma(M) + \gamma(N)) = \gamma_1(P) \cdot \gamma(M \oplus N)$$ 
$$= \gamma(P \otimes (M \oplus N)) = \gamma((P \otimes M) \oplus (P \otimes N))$$ 
$$= \gamma(P \otimes M) + \gamma(P \otimes N) = \gamma_1(P)\cdot \gamma(M) + \gamma_1(P) \cdot \gamma(N),$$
as required.

Let $M,N$ be flat, finitely generated $A$-modules, and let $P$ be a finitely generated $A$-module. Then,
$$(\gamma_1(M) + \gamma_1(N)) \cdot \gamma(P) = \gamma_1(M \oplus N) \cdot \gamma(P)$$ 
$$= \gamma((M \oplus N) \otimes P) = \gamma((M \otimes P) \oplus (N \otimes P))$$ 
$$= \gamma(M \otimes P) + \gamma(N \otimes P) = \gamma_1(M) \cdot \gamma(P) + \gamma_1(N) \cdot \gamma(P),$$
as required.

Finally, suppose $M,N$ are flat, finitely generated $A$-modules and $P$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. Then,
$$\gamma_1(M)\cdot(\gamma_1(N) \cdot \gamma(P)) = \gamma_1(M)\cdot(\gamma(N \otimes P)) = \gamma(M \otimes (N \otimes P))$$
$$= \gamma((M \otimes N) \otimes P) = \gamma_1(M \otimes N) \cdot \gamma(P) = (\gamma_1(M) \cdot \gamma_1(N)) \cdot \gamma(P)$$
since, as in the proof of (i), $M \otimes N$ is a flat and finitely generated $A$-module. Thus, $K(A)$ has a $K_1(A)$-module structure
in this way.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(iii) Proof.]
Suppose $A$ is a Noetherian local ring. Then, if $M$ is a flat, finitely generated $A$-module, then $M$ is free by Exercise \ref{7.15}. Thus, $M \cong A^n$ as an 
$A$-module. Hence, $\gamma_1(M) = \gamma_1(A^n) = n\gamma_1(A)$. Thus, $K_1(A)$ is a free abelian group with one generator, $\gamma_1(A)$, so $K_1(A) \cong \Z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(iv) Proof.]
Let $f : A \to B$ be a ring homomorphism, where $A$ and $B$ are Noetherian. Let $M$ be a flat, finitely generated $A$-module. Then, if $\{m_i\}$ are the finitely 
many generators, then $\{1 \otimes m_i\}$ generate $M_B = B \otimes_A M$ as a $B$-module, so $M_B$ is finitely generated. Suppose $N_1 \to N_2$ is an injection
of $B$-modules. Then, we can view $N_1$ and $N_2$ as $A$-modules via $f$, so since $M$ is flat, $M \otimes_A N_1 \to M \otimes_A N_2$ is injective. However, we
can rewrite this map:
$$M \otimes_A (B \otimes_B N_1) \to M \otimes_A (B \otimes_B N_2)$$
$$(M \otimes_A B) \otimes_B N_1 \to (M \otimes_A B) \otimes_B N_2$$
$$M_B \otimes_B N_1 \to M_B \otimes_B N_2.$$
Thus, the final map, being identical to $M \otimes_A N_1 \to M \otimes_A N_2$, is also injective, so $M_B$ is a flat, finitely generated $B$-module. Thus, we have a 
homomorphism $C_1(A) \to C_1(B)$ from the free abelian group of isomorphism classes of flat, finitely generated $A$-modules to those of $B$. 

This becomes a homomorphism $C_1(A) \to K_1(B)$. Now, suppose we have a short exact sequence
$$0 \to N' \to N \to N'' \to 0$$
of flat, finitely generated $A$-modules. This gives rise to a short exact sequence
$$\Tor_1^A(B,N'') \to N_B' \to N_B \to N_B'' \to 0.$$
By Exercise \ref{2.24}, $\Tor_1^A(B,N'') = 0$ since $N''$ is flat. Thus, short exact sequences map to short exact sequences, so $D_1(A) \subset \ker(C_1(A) \to K_1(A))$.
Hence, our homomorphism lifts to a homomorphism $f^! : K_1(A) \to K_1(B)$.

Finally, suppose $g : B \to C$ is another ring homomorphism (with $C$ Noetherian). To see that $(g \circ f)^! = g^! \circ f^!$, first note the following commutative
diagram:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
C_1(A) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & C_1(B) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & C_1(C) \arrow[d] \\
K_1(A) \arrow[r, "f^!"] & K_1(B) \arrow[r, "g^!"] & K_1(C).
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Thus, it is sufficient to show that the composite map $C_1(A) \to C_1(B) \to C_1(C)$ is identical to the map $C_1(A) \to C_1(C)$ coming from $g \circ f$. Let $M$ be an
$A$-module. Under $f$, this gives rise to a $B$-module $B \otimes_A M$. Under $g$, this gives rise to a $C$-module $C \otimes_B (B \otimes_A M)$. But we have the isomorphism
$$C \otimes_B (B \otimes_A M) \cong (C \otimes_B B) \otimes_A M \cong C \otimes_A M,$$
which is precisely the extension of scalars of $M$ for $A$ straight to $C$ via $g \circ f$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[(v) Proof.]
Let $f : A \to B$ be a finite ring homomorphism. Then, $B$ is a finitely generated $A$-module so that $\gamma_A(B) \in K(A)$. Now, suppose $M \in K_1(A)$ and $N \in K(B)$.
We have
$$f_!(f^!(\gamma_{1,A}(M)) \cdot \gamma_B(N)) = f_!(\gamma_{1,B}(M_B) \cdot \gamma_B(N)) = f_!(\gamma_B(M_B \otimes_B N))$$
$$= \gamma_A(M_B \otimes_B N) = \gamma_A((M \otimes_A B) \otimes_B N) = \gamma_A(M \otimes_A (B \otimes_B N))$$
$$= \gamma_A(M \otimes_A N_A) = \gamma_{1,A}(M) \cdot \gamma_A(N_A) = \gamma_{1,A}(M) \cdot f_!(\gamma_B(N)).$$
In other words, regarding $K(B)$ as a $K_1(A)$-module by restriction of scalars, the homomorphism $f^!$ is a $K_1(A)$-module homomorphism.
\end{proof}
